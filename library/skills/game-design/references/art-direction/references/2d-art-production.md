# Size a 2D scene for the player’s decision

## One shift at the parcel belt

A night sorter diverts diamond-stamped parcels into a padded chute. Round-stamped parcels continue straight ahead. The creator wants the depot to feel like an old two-colour print. Give the stationary machine a slightly uneven ink edge. Give the moving parcels a broad, quiet paper face so the route stamp stays clear. The artistic decision is where the ink can break up without making a diamond look like a circle.

**Frame the choice.** Sketch the belt approaching its fork. For this study, use a 640 × 360 play image showing nine tile heights. A square tile occupies 40 × 40 image pixels. A parcel one and a half tiles wide occupies 60 pixels across. Draw both stamp shapes inside that actual footprint. At three tiles of belt travel per second, three visible tile widths between the first fully readable stamp and the fork give the player one second to act. Cover the HUD area on the sketch. Try a 480 × 360 crop and measure the distance again. If the crop loses the first readable tile, move the fork or change the framing before detailing the boxes.

**Set the art budget.** Give one artist a day for the first station. Draw one repeating belt section, a base and hinged arm for the fork, two box bodies, two route stamps, and a lamp housing. Put the belt below the parcels and attach each stamp to its box. Give the fork arm its own pivot. Put the chute lip in front only where a parcel has already committed to its route. Run a short and a tall box through both lanes. A stamp that disappears behind the lip too early calls for an overlap change. A stamp that breaks up on the open belt calls for a stroke or texture change.

**Spend animation and lighting work where the eye uses it.** Rotate the rigid arm around its pin. For a parcel bursting open after a wrong turn, sketch three replacement drawings of the flap as its broad face turns edge-on. Compare them with one hinged drawing at the most extreme pose. Keep the drawings if that narrow silhouette sells the mishap. Record the minutes to add a second box size with each method. For the lamp, compare a fixed patch of ink on the belt with a moving highlight on the parcel. Build the moving-light trial from an evenly lit box so a painted directional shadow cannot contradict it. Keep the fixed pool if the highlight washes out the stamp just before the fork.

**Run a platform test.** On the intended lowest-spec device, use the normal HUD, the narrowest supported view and a full belt. Give an unfamiliar player both stamp types. Record when they can first name the route and whether they move the fork in time. Someone who names the route correctly but moves too late suggests a timing or control problem. Someone who repeatedly confuses the shapes at the lamp gives the artist a specific frame to revise. Replay the same busy segment with each light treatment. Compare frame times and resident texture memory under the same conditions. For a 60 Hz target a refresh lasts about 16.7 ms. Check the slow frames at the fork, where a hitch costs decision time.

## Apply the budget to the next room

| A production choice | A small comparison that can change it |
|---|---|
| Repeat a surface or draw a unique one | Make a straight belt, a bend and a junction. Move the camera across all three at the closest and most distant supported views. Check gaps, repeated marks lining up across seams and whether the junction is clear on its own. |
| Draw each pose or reuse hinged parts | Pick the deepest bend, the longest reach and one second costume. Keep the contact point in the same place as the body changes. Layer the actor in front of and behind another one. If parts interleave or the cloth folds through a limb, try a single replacement drawing for the problem pose and compare the cost. |
| Separate visible art from collision and triggers | Put a crate with a decorative lip on a continuous floor. Give it a simple collision shape where the player should stop. Move a short and a tall crate across a tile boundary. Test the route again after removing a floor tile. Check that a detection area registers entry while letting the parcel through. |
| Paint light or move it | Compare a baked ink patch, a moving highlight and a small contact shadow. Put a parcel at the onset of each effect and at the fork. Keep the option that preserves the stamp and the sense of a box on a belt. Measure it with a full lane. |
| Sort flat layers and props with depth | Cross the same aisle in front of a shelf, behind it and through an open doorway. Inspect where an actor or movable prop passes the shelf's edges. Compare a layer-order change with a local mask. |
| Use drawn frames, particles or a shader | For a paper burst, block the silhouette with three drawings. Compare it with a few particles and a small shader preview. Watch the cue on the next parcel during the effect. Time the art for one new material, then compare slow frames on the target device. |

Keep the smallest asset list that makes the next decision possible to test. For a busy frame, inspect which layers are drawn, how many cover the same space, and which textures remain resident. Choose the cut that improves the measured cost while keeping the cue visible. A larger atlas or another effect layer is worth its work when it buys something apparent at the chosen camera and play speed.
