# Give a palette a job

The player carries a film reel into a dark projection booth. Two projectors face the screen. One is showing a film. The player must thread the reel onto the idle machine before the next changeover. Its empty spindle is easy to recognize up close. From the door, the first clue is a raised metal flag. The artist wants the light spilling through the projection windows to remain the most beautiful thing in the room. How little paint does the flag need?

## Change one property at a time

*Hue* names the color family, *value* describes how light or dark a surface looks, and *chroma* describes how far its color departs from a neutral gray of the same value. Keep a neutral gray beside each swatch and compare them against the color actually behind the object. A useful match is close enough in the rendered view that the group can judge one difference at a time. Some hues can stay vivid in a dark patch more easily than others. When a hue change also changes apparent lightness, make a separate comparison that controls for that change.

## Paint three versions of the same flag

Freeze the view at the booth door. Start with muted red-brown paint against the dark iron housing. Make three copies of the frame. Keep the size and pose of the flag, the lighting, and the surrounding materials fixed.

| Change | Paint decision | What to look for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hue | Shift the flag toward blue-green. Match its original apparent lightness and color strength. | Can the cooler paint distinguish the idle machine from both warm window reflections? |
| Value | Keep the reddish hue and muted chroma. Lighten the flag against the housing. | Does its raised outline become visible from the door, including in a grayscale copy of the rendered frame? |
| Chroma | Keep the hue and apparent value. Give the flag a stronger red. | Does the patch attract attention while still looking like painted metal in a quiet booth? |

Judge those matches in the rendered view. The number labeled V in an HSV picker gives the largest RGB channel. Two hues at the same HSV value can look different in lightness. Check rendered grayscale and the full-color view. Treat the first as a quick test for apparent light and dark and the second as the view where the player actually decides.

## Check the choice in motion

Try each candidate from the normal camera at the smallest supported play size. Approach during a bright passage of film, then during a dark one. Give a fresh player the next reel and watch which machine they reach for first. Switch the running projector and repeat with another player so the correct answer changes sides. Ask what told them the spindle was free and what they remember about the room.

If the windows disappear from their account, reduce the painted area to a stripe on the flag’s raised edge. If the stripe vanishes when the film brightens, try a darker backing directly behind it. If players can find the stripe but choose the running machine, show the empty spindle through a cutout in the raised flag. That connects the cue to the action the player needs to take.

## Give an accent enough area to be seen

Paint the same strong red on a full flag, one broad stripe and a thin edge. Compare all three inside the frame during a bright scene on the screen. The larger patch may compete with the windows even if its swatch looked right. A thin edge may vanish in the doorway view. Use the smallest area that remains useful at the time of the decision. If both projectors still look ready, compare the raised and lowered shapes with the paint removed. Keep the state visible in the shape or a plain label and use the accent to guide the eye there.

In another room the creator may want viewers to discover the mechanism slowly. For free exploration, compare the cue at the distance where the player can examine it. For a changeover that will leave a blank screen, compare it at the earliest distance where the player has time to act. Write down the result for both the active and idle machines, including a frame in which their lighting reverses.
