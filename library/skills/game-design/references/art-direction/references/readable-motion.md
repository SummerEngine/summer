# Give a tin frog a readable jump

In a side-view toy shop, the player jumps a wind-up frog between boxes. Holding the button winds the spring. Releasing it sends the frog as far as the stored tension allows. The player facing a wide gap needs to recognize a fully wound frog. The artist wants it to feel like a painted toy with stiff brass legs and a surprisingly lively kick. Those two intentions can share one drawing.

## Draw the extremes

Draw the frog standing and fully wound, both at game size on the same pencil baseline. Keep the back toe planted. In the standing drawing there is daylight under the belly. In the wound drawing the belly almost touches the box and the folded knee sticks up above the shell. Cover the painted markings and look again. If the knee disappears against a shelf behind it, shift the outline or the shelf until the two poses read across the room.

## Give the press and release different timing

On the first visible update after a press, dip the frog. Try dropping the belly three pixels, then two, then one at equal time intervals on the way to full tension. The dwindling spaces make the last part look hard to compress. Keep the fully wound drawing on screen for as long as the button stays down. A held drawing now tells the player that waiting longer buys no distance.

Release from **any** depth. Open the legs and start the jump on that update, using the charge already earned. The short tap gets a low hop even if the artist had planned another crouch drawing. Once airborne, fold the legs up under the shell. On landing, plant the feet and borrow the shallow crouch for one beat. Let another press start winding from contact. Keeping control during the landing makes the frog feel nimble even with a deliberate hold before a long leap.

## Spend drawings where the eye looks

Paint the shell, eye, and maker's mark together once. Move that piece above hinged leg shapes. Budget one special drawing for the kick, when the overlapping feet separate. Align the repeated pieces to the same pivot. The toy's hinges give the artist a place to hide a join.

Ask someone to cross a narrow gap followed by a wide one with the sound off. Watch when they release. If they keep waiting after full charge, try raising the folded knee farther. If a quick tap feels swallowed, compare input, takeoff and first visible movement on the same timeline. Add a drawing only after establishing which event the player missed.

## Compare the movement the player will repeat

- **Contact and weight.** Trace the back toe through pressing, full charge and release against a guide placed on the box. The toe should stay planted until the push. Try a slipping toe only if a slip is meant to change how the player reads the force. Do the same check for the foot that first touches on landing.
- **Spacing and holds.** Compare an evenly spaced crouch with one that covers more distance at the start. Freeze at full charge in each. Give a fresh player one narrow gap and one wide one. Ask when they thought the frog was ready and compare their answer with the input and position trace.
- **Reuse and seams.** Hold the shell fixed in an idle loop while one leg ticks. Follow the tip of that leg from the last drawing into the first. Then preview the loop and the first tap against the real shop background. If the resting movement makes a tap hard to see, shorten it or move it away from the part of the silhouette that communicates charging.
- **Restarts and interruptions.** Press during the landing and tap again as soon as the frog makes contact. If the written and visible response disagree, align the first meaningful change with the actual rule. Keep a dedicated drawing when overlapping pieces cannot show the contact that needs to read.
