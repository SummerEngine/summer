# Exploration walkthroughs

These are fictional game situations and proposed tests. Each example starts with a concrete observation to look for. The comparisons give criteria for making a decision after inspection or play.

| Moment under review | Case |
|---|---|
| A choice silently closes a required lead | [The last ferry](#the-last-ferry) |
| A new ability works but players cannot return to use it | [The wind shutters](#the-wind-shutters) |
| Players try every object after reading the clue | [The shadow booth](#the-shadow-booth) |
| A correct suspect still produces a failed accusation | [The observatory hearing](#the-observatory-hearing) |
| A safe-looking path fails only at the edge of the view | [The cistern drop](#the-cistern-drop) |

## The last ferry

A one-way ferry takes players out of the village. At the island gate they learn it accepts a ferryman's stamp. The stamp was on an optional desk before boarding. A recording shows one player retracing the island route three times.

- **Competing explanations.** Boarding without the stamp may create an impossible state. The gate may instead accept another credential, but its response suggests the only useful action is to return. An accessible stamp can still be an unfair requirement if the first indication it matters arrives on the island.
- **Discriminate.** Follow the earliest legal boarding route with an empty inventory, then try to acquire or substitute the stamp from the island. If the stamp is truly mandatory, compare fresh players on that route with and without a faded stamp-shaped mark on the ferry notice, visible before boarding. Preserve the crossing and any other use for the desk. Ask immediately after boarding what made them stay to search, leave, or disregard the notice. A player who reads the mark as decoration still lacks a reason to search.
- **Decide.** For a continuous adventure, resolve the unreachable gate by making the stamp recoverable or part of boarding. Once progress is possible from every legal arrival, use the notice to support a deliberate search and keep the one-way trip. If an alternative credential works but remains undiscovered, tune the gate response to acknowledge the type of proof it accepts. If players knowingly travel unprepared and can learn the route on a short, intended loop, preserve that discovery.
- **Interview turn.** If the creator says the stranding is intentional, ask “On the next loop, what can a player do differently before the ferry leaves?” If continuity is the promise, ask “From the island, which action recovers the required proof?”

For a paper check, start once on the dock and once on the island with blank inventory. From each start, write every legal path to the stamp or to a substitute. If the island start has no such path, the notice can reduce the frequency of stranding. To guarantee progress after a legal boarding, include the stamp in boarding or add an island recovery path.

## The wind shutters

An old mill has a long south passage with brass shutters. Players see the closed shutters, climb two flights, and acquire a wind tool in the loft, where they use it on a vane. Players then return through the hub and pass the south entrance repeatedly. The creator proposes an objective arrow.

- **Competing explanations.** The player remembers the shutters and sees the tool's relevance but confuses the south and east passages on the return. Or the demonstration reads as moving exposed vanes only, and the player has no reason to revisit a shutter. A player who finds the shutters and opens them may simply dislike the trip after making the correct decision.
- **Discriminate.** At the loft exit, use a fresh player's first turn and, immediately after, ask which old obstacle they wanted to try. If they nominate the shutters but turn east, compare one version with the waterwheel audible or visible from the south passage in both directions. If they nominate only the vanes, leave the route alone and place the same etched vane shape on one shutter. Watch whether players predict the relationship before approaching. Once they can predict and navigate, compare the existing descent with a loft stair that opens from above and reconnects to the old hub. Time the part of the return after the route choice and ask what players noticed along the way.
- **Decide.** Tune the landmark if players already understand the tool and first-time wrong turns specifically fall. Tune the shared visual detail if it changes what players predict. Keep the initial blocked passage if the later re-reading is satisfying. Cut a solved stretch of return if it adds only walking. Preserve a return segment that enables a new use of the tool, a new crossing decision, or a reveal players value.
- **Interview turn.** If the creator has no recording, ask “At the moment they get the tool, what's the first old barrier they can reasonably think it fits?” Once that is established, ask about the return fork on a later turn.

Mark where the loft shortcut rejoins the outbound path. Run one version that emerges at the recognizable waterwheel and another that emerges at a new junction. Keep the travel time equal. If players find the shutters only when they return by the waterwheel, the shortcut works best when it reconnects to something they already know.

## The shadow booth

A festival keeper wants something that can play the moon without speaking. The intended move is to hang a painted paper disk on a hook in front of a lamp. One player drags the disk to several points on the lamp before finding the hook. A second player lists every round item in their bag.

- **Competing explanations.** The first player may understand shadow casting but reasonably try the lamp where the game only accepts the mounting hook. The second may know the joke but have no reason to choose a material the light can outline. In one language the line may cue a theatrical role, while another translation suggests a quiet person.
- **Discriminate.** Before giving feedback, get the attempted action and the change the player expects. For a sound plan, replay the input with and without the disk and lamp conditions. Compare making the hook briefly acknowledge interaction with mapping the reasonable lamp-target drag to the existing mount when its conditions hold. For missing reasoning, show a paper star already making a shadow in that booth, leaving the disk and interaction intact. In each shipped language under review, ask a new player to give the keeper's request in their own words and choose an item on a fresh attempt. Measure the kind of first attempt and its reasoning alongside completion.
- **Decide.** Accept an equivalent gesture when the target and consequence are clear from the player's expression. Give a property-specific response to a plausible wrong object, such as a glass bead letting the light through. If fresh players can identify the hook but still sweep inventory, tune the physical clue. Keep the wordplay if the scene can give a second route to the same inference. A vocabulary game can deliberately require word knowledge when the audience and purpose support it. An escape room that promises shared reasoning should let the group express a sound theory through the available props.
- **Interview turn.** If the creator says everyone gets there eventually, ask “What do players say the disk will do before their first drag?” If they already say it casts the moon, ask what target they selected when that attempt failed.

Prepare three cards for the booth with the same request and different replies. A lamp drag can name the hook. A glass bead can cast a faint spot while the reply points out that light passes through it. A correct paper disk can cast the moon. Ask players to use the response from their first try to plan one more action. If they can now explain the material they need yet still drag it to the lamp, fix the accepted target or the reply before adding clues.

When inventing another puzzle for the world, begin with a property the player can observe and reuse. Sketch how paper blocks the booth's light, how a cutout shapes the shadow, and how moving the lamp moves that shape. Let the player test one consequence before it matters, through a working prop or traces of earlier use. Then give that property a new purpose, such as moving the lamp to align a cutout with a missing section of a mural. Try the new action before and after learning the clue. If the challenge is making the connection, allow the effect in either order. Require a later conversation when it changes permission, access, or some other fact that affects the action.

## The observatory hearing

Only the astronomer and the keeper can turn the observatory beacon, and they can do so only at the tower console. The beacon turned at 9:10. The astronomer claims both caretakers were in the lift from 9:08 to 9:12. The lift log records who was inside throughout each trip and lists only the keeper for that interval. A player names the astronomer but fails the formal accusation several times. The UI asks them to challenge a witness statement with an item.

- **Competing explanations.** They recognize an ominous character and guess correctly without being able to distinguish the caretakers. They might have a valid inference but match an index entry to the whole witness statement when the game expects an attached log on a specific sentence. They might also believe a witness saw the lantern turn when the witness only heard it reported.
- **Discriminate.** Before any verdict, ask which observed fact rules out the keeper and which claim it addresses. If the player says the lift log places only the keeper away from the console at 9:10, test how the game responds to presenting the log's index entry or attached record against the astronomer's claim about both caretakers. If they only say the astronomer seemed suspicious, check whether they ever learned that the log records occupancy for the whole interval. Add that detail only if it is missing. Test both legal clue orders with fresh players. If the inference depends on who knew what, record who saw the event and when information could pass between characters. Change one line to distinguish direct witnessing from hearsay and check whether it changes the player's explanation.
- **Decide.** Keep the narrow challenge when a specific contradiction has a real purpose, and tune pairing feedback when players can name it yet submit it in a reasonable unsupported way. Add discriminating evidence only when no existing clue excludes the rival by the time of commitment. Cut a retry that charges players solely for figuring out a mislabeled evidence slot. If the experience is an ambiguous character study, play can support several defensible readings. Test whether the final response engages with the reason the player offers.
- **Interview turn.** After “They know who did it,” ask “What could they point to that makes the other caretaker's account impossible?” If they have an answer, ask which part the game requires them to submit when the conversation reaches that branch.

Make a second hearing with the same two permissions and timestamp but reverse which caretaker was in the lift. Give the keeper the claim that both were in the lift. Keep the accusation interaction identical. Before permitting a submission, ask the player to identify the record and which person it rules out. If their reasoning changes with the log and their submission still fails, focus the next trial on pairing evidence with a statement.

## The cistern drop

In a side-view adventure a player steps down an old shaft. They can change sides only near the top. A shelf is visible at the bottom of the screen just before they fall out of view. The creator calls this a jump problem.

- **Competing explanations.** The screen may reveal the shelf after the last steerable frame. The camera boundary might itself end the attempt even when a physical ledge could catch the character. If the ledge is visible in time and the death plane is elsewhere, the controller or jump geometry may be the cause.
- **Discriminate.** Trace the last frame that allows steering, the first frame that shows the shelf, and the exact event that stops play. If the camera also drives death or respawn, make a static preview from the top or let the camera look down without moving those boundaries. Test one ledge with the same flight and a known landing side. Ask a fresh player to indicate their intended side before stepping off. If earlier visibility changes their plan and where they land, tune the view. If they choose correctly and miss, compare the accepted input and actual trajectory before changing the frame.
- **Decide.** Keep the downward reveal when anticipation is the intended choice and there is a safe way to probe. For a precision drop, tune the point of preview if players otherwise commit blindly. If the camera is an active boundary and the player predicts a safe fall into a region that actually kills them, communicate the boundary or give the shelf the promised physical function. A game about fragmented views may make reconstructing space the puzzle. A game about precise movement can expose the landing area and still require execution.
- **Interview turn.** Ask “At the last moment the player could steer toward it, what of the shelf can they see?” If visible, ask what the game recorded as the reason for the failed landing.

To check the boundary, run the same descent twice with the same steering inputs and shift only the viewport. Log what ended each attempt. If moving the viewport changes the death time, the camera is part of the traversal rule. Run a separate preview test with that rule fixed before deciding whether visibility is the main problem.

## The evening delivery

A lighthouse keeper leaves a cup by a radio before making rounds. On the way they pass a glass booth with a lock and a boathouse with a passage to shore. The radio later goes dead and the objective names both ways to reach safety. A designer proposes waiting to name the choices.

- **Competing explanations.** The prompt might make players treat the building as a checklist and miss an optional change in the familiar radio. It might instead free attention to choose between the booth and shore under pressure. If they know both options but cannot get to either, inspect how the earlier errand taught the routes. If they can reach both and always choose one, inspect what each branch offers.
- **Discriminate.** Record whether the player uses or sees both approaches on the first errand and what the game says when the radio goes quiet. With fresh players, compare the named choices at that moment with one short reason to act and the same exact choices on request. Keep the room layout, route availability, time pressure, cue prominence, and recall controls fixed. After the attempt ask which actions they thought were possible, what detail informed their choice, and whether any uncertainty felt worth exploring. Check their first turn and whether a wrong turn can be recovered before failure. If both variants yield the same plans and discovery, test placement of the optional detail or the usability of the branches.
- **Decide.** Keep the named objective when it makes two viable actions legible and the suspense lies in their consequences. Try the shorter cue if both actions are already recognizable and the explicit choices are displacing a discovery the creator values. Try a safe outbound errand that teaches the landmark when players can name the booth yet cannot find it under pressure. A goal can guide a player through an authored opening while optional details reward looking past it. A timed encounter needs a different basis for optional help than an untimed puzzle players can stop to think about.
- **Interview turn.** Ask “When the radio stops, what should the player have to work out for themselves?” If the answer is which route is safer, ask what evidence lets them make that choice. If the answer is which actions exist, ask where they could have learned one of those actions during the rounds.

Put one optional clue on the first errand, such as a second name scratched on the radio's case. Give both versions the same delivery objective and the same chance to see the radio. After the outage, ask which parts of the room players considered relevant. If explicit choices help players take a route and they still notice the scratch, the immediate objective can coexist with that discovery.

## The lantern warden

A fictional warden walks around a ruin. At the approach, the player can wait behind a pillar, see a sweep of the lantern, and hear the warden's key ring before choosing when to cross. On video a player rushes, fails, then rushes again after a long return.

- **Competing explanations.** The player may never notice which direction the warden is moving. They might already have a plan, but detection from the same-looking position could be governed by an unseen change in alert state. They might understand the risk and rush to avoid replaying the long approach.
- **Discriminate.** Replay the crossing from the same save and check the current threat state and what event caused detection. Give one version a glimpse of the sweep from behind the pillar early enough to use it. If the jingle carries direction, test a timed optional caption conveying that same direction at the original time. Compare plans from the approach and ask after the attempt which uncertainty mattered. If attempts begin with coherent plans and only later devolve into rushing, compare the original retry with a checkpoint just before the decision. Keep the approach for the first encounter and the same warden state at the checkpoint.
- **Decide.** Tune an actionable cue if players begin to choose when to cross and still describe an unresolved risk they value. If they can predict the sweep and reproduce an unexplained detection, inspect the world rule or clarify what changed. Shorten the retry if it produces another deliberate attempt without losing the buildup players value. Keep the return when it contains a meaningful choice or players describe it as central to the dread. A stealth scoring challenge can promise exact prediction and warrant precise detection feedback. A horror game can leave the full detection threshold hidden while giving players a reason to wait or move.
- **Interview turn.** If the creator wants more fear, ask “What can a player safely learn about the warden before moving?” If players already state a plan, ask “What did they believe caused the last detection?”

Test the alternative cue from the player's actual cover. At the instant the keys sound offscreen, show a brief direction cue. When the warden moves past the pillar, compare the direction players predict with and without audio. If a caption conveys the event without enough direction to support the same plan, revise it before judging the tension of the crossing.

## The singing shore

In an untimed island game, rocks add a musical phrase as players pass. Some visitors wander once and then say there was nothing to do. Others return unaided to the rock arch at dusk. The creator is considering a permanent list of places.

- **Competing explanations.** A player may want to return but fail to recognize an approach. They may know how to return and still have no reason to choose a place. They may expect a quest from the opening even though the game promises listening and discovery.
- **Discriminate.** Ask what the player was trying to do when they stopped or opened the map. For a named destination, expose one recognizable landmark from both sides of the approach. After an optional detour, see if the player can resume the journey with occasional reorientation. For players who can return but see no point, keep the same map and let a known place add one distinct phrase at dusk. See whether they notice the change, choose a return, or describe a different moment they value. For a mismatched opening promise, try one opening encounter where moving closer and farther away changes what they hear. Keep the rest of the route identical.
- **Decide.** Keep the bounded space and optional wandering when players choose their own purpose and recover from detours. Tune landmark visibility when return attempts fail. Add a local change when players can navigate and a new state makes revisiting worthwhile. If a particular destination is mandatory and the world cannot provide timely orientation, test an optional pointer there. A trading hub may value getting to the player-selected destination quickly, while an exploratory soundscape can value hearing what lies in between.
- **Interview turn.** Ask “When players call it empty, what were they trying to find or do just before they stopped?” If they name a place, pursue recovery. If they name a task they expected, ask what the opening taught them to expect.

Draw two return routes to the arch and include one detour on each. Put a single recognizable landmark at the point where they rejoin. First check whether players can find the arch after a detour. Then keep the route fixed and compare what they can hear at dusk. A landmark can help them return, and the new phrase can give them a reason to do so. Check both before replacing the soundscape with a list of destinations.

## The prism seedlings

A logic garden asks players to split light before feeding two seedlings. The first board clears almost every time. A second board with the branches mirrored stalls those same players.

- **Competing explanations.** The starting mirror angle may accidentally feed both seedlings via reflected light, letting players finish without making the intended decision. They may actively use the splitter on the first board but see only an all-green success signal, with no evidence for which branch the splitter feeds. The second board might add an unrelated camera or placement problem.
- **Discriminate.** Try the first board without touching the splitter. Then replay from the same starting state, change just one mirror, and compare which seedling responds. If a bypass exists, close it on a copy and keep the feedback identical. If use is already required, make the affected light branch react visibly when the player changes it. On an unfamiliar board keep placement difficulty constant and ask the player which seedling will light before moving the mirror. Record their subsequent action as well as their answer.
- **Decide.** Require the splitter on a compact practice board if an observed bypass prevents the promised lesson. Tune feedback if use was already required and prediction improves after the visual change. Keep an intentional alternate solution on an open garden board when it demonstrates useful reasoning of its own. Test a control or view change if a player predicts correctly and cannot perform the placement.
- **Interview turn.** If the creator uses clear rate as proof of learning, ask “Can this board clear without the player changing the light split?” If it cannot, ask what a player can see change in response to the move.

Use a fresh board with two reachable settings and one plausible but unlit branch. Ask which seedling will receive light after a single rotation, then let the player change the setting and check their prediction. A clear completed with the untouched splitter establishes a bypass. A prediction that matches the changed branch gives evidence about the rule the learner can use.

## The shifting vault

A generated ruin has new corridors every run. One seed stranded a playtester. On the other seeds they take the near branch at every first junction. The generator authors are considering more room themes.

- **Competing explanations.** The failed seed may have placed a key past its own gate during spatial assembly. The recurring choice on valid seeds may be caused by a dominant route that yields equal treasure more safely. Alternatively, the costly branch may already have a distinct reward that is impossible to read from the fork.
- **Discriminate.** Save the failing seed and repeat with a fixed loadout. Walk reachable states from the mandatory start, preserving keys consumed, one-way links and tool use. Find the first stage at which the path becomes impossible, and replay that stage after the smallest relevant change. For the repetitive choice, use a fixed sample of valid seeds. At the first consequential fork, record which alternatives are actually reachable, what they cost, and what a player can foresee before entry. Compare the frequency of meaningful tradeoffs across the sample by their expected loss and potential reward. This is a quick diagnostic, with a larger representative sample needed to characterize a generator's output. On one repeated seed, expose the existing reward from the fork if the alternatives already differ. If cost and reward are redundant, make one branch trade time or a scarce fictional tool for a distinct payoff and keep the terrain and start state.
- **Decide.** Fix the structural impossibility on the mandatory route and preserve deliberately inaccessible optional niches. Tune a cue if revealing an existing tradeoff changes the player's forecast and choice. Change the generator's branch constraints if different shapes keep offering the same dominant action. Keep a consistent first choice if the opening is for orientation and later play provides the promised variety. Visual themes serve an exploration goal when players use them to recognize a place or look forward to a reveal. They solve a different problem from repeated mechanical choices.
- **Interview turn.** Ask “Pick two seeds that look different. What does the player gain and give up at the first fork in each?” If the creator names a stranded seed, start with “Which action can the player still take when they first reach the gate with no key?”

For the stranded seed, write the intended sequence on cards. Acquire the key, open the gate, reach the exit. Place the cards on the actual rooms and trace them from the entrance. If the order works on paper but fails on the map, check placement and one-way links first. For the valid seeds, ask what looked worth the cost at the fork. Pair the answer with the traced costs to distinguish a hidden tradeoff from a repeated dominant choice.
