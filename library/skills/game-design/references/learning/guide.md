# Learning and training

Use the creator's promise and the evidence already available. If called during an interview, continue from their latest answer with one relevant question and wait for their reply. Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## Design a first playable round

Start with a specific learner and a plausible situation. For a skill, finish the sentence “With [these facts and tools], the learner can [take this action].” Name the typical error and write the choice that would expose it. For curiosity, name an uncertainty the player can follow through more than one lead. Decide what voluntary evidence to watch for, such as comparing two sources, recording a prediction, or following up on a contradiction. Use the promise to decide how much of the rule to provide up front. Rehearsal can start with the explicit rule. Inquiry needs accessible evidence and room to choose what to test.

**Draft a one-page prototype.** Ask the creator or subject expert what the learner already knows and what decision they should be able to make alone. Then write the opening situation, the evidence and tools available, two plausible actions and their consequences. List what the facilitator needs to prepare and when to offer help. Give a second facilitator the page and see whether they can run one round without clarifying a rule with you. Use their questions to revise the setup. Afterward ask the learner what evidence supported their choice and what could change it.

**Choose when to show the exact instruction.** Make the role, available actions and route to help legible from the start. Show a routine goal or a confirmed change promptly when the player needs it to take part. If the point is to interpret a discrepancy, give the learner a chance to examine the clues first. A journal or help card can supply a neutral recap, retain a message the learner needs to compare, or give the next action on request. In the museum sorting exercise below, a hint can invite the learner to compare the material fields. The next help card can explain what a blank field means if they ask.

For a first exploratory round, record which leads learners pursue and where they lose the thread. To compare timing, change when the exact instruction appears automatically and keep the clues and requested help reachable in both versions. Keep the delay if more players compare evidence independently and still reach the decision. Restore earlier guidance if players chiefly lose their bearings or miss the consequential change. If timing makes little difference, inspect whether the clues give players a comparison worth making.

## Inspect the game

For skill practice, pick one decision and follow it from what the learner can perceive to what the game accepts and explains. Ask the creator or subject expert to name the action, relevant evidence, and conditions for success. Distinguish a prerequisite from the outcome. “Classify unfamiliar objects using their catalog records” gives you a decision to inspect. “Fill the cabinet” gives you the game goal. For open inquiry, follow one voluntary lead or unscored comparison and record what it lets the learner observe, test, or revisit. In either case, record the scene, rule, or timestamp.

**Try to win while skipping the judgment.** Follow a color or icon, memorize position, retry until accepted, or copy the strongest teammate, as the rules allow. A successful bypass shows that the judgment is optional on that route. To see whether learners actually rely on the bypass, change the surface cue with the rule fixed. Then keep that cue fixed and change the evidence so a different action is correct. Record which cue predicts their first choice. In a team game, collect a private prediction and reason from each learner before discussion. Then have the team choose together and note whether the members introduce new evidence or follow the first answer given.

**Preserve the decision when simplifying.** Keep the cue, consequence, and any time or coordination constraint that belongs to the learning goal. In a team scheduling exercise, give each person a shift card and preserve the time it takes to get an answer from a partner if coordinating coverage is the skill. For suspected input friction, let the learner state or point to their choice before executing it. If a learner gives the right action and reason but cannot submit it, test an accessible input or presentation with the same decision. If they can access the evidence and still select the wrong rule, inspect the practice and feedback.

## Interview the creator

Start with the first uncertainty that could change the recommendation.

- If the outcome is broad, ask “What should a learner be able to decide on their own in a new situation?”
- If the outcome is clear, ask “In which turn can I see the player make that decision?”
- If the player can win by following a cue or another player, ask “What would distinguish someone using the intended rule from someone copying that answer?”
- If the creator points to high scores, ask “What new example would make a surface cue and the intended rule point to different answers?”
- If the promise is curiosity or rehearsal, ask “What would you want to see the learner investigate or attempt during play?”

## Make the decision

An unfamiliar but comparable case checks for near transfer. Record the first independent decision and reason before any scoring, hints, peer answers, or feedback. Agree with the creator on acceptable actions and evidence in advance. Accept pointing or selecting the relevant evidence when explaining it aloud is outside the goal. Report what the learner can currently apply in those conditions. To attribute improvement to the game or a change, take a comparable starting measure and use a comparison with similar practice time and content. Assign learners to versions by chance within similar starting levels when practical. Track practiced-case completion, control access, and voluntary replay separately. For retention, check again after the relevant delay. For application in another setting, observe the judgment in that setting.

### Worked example of museum collection sorting

**Set up the paper round.** Label three spaces Wood, Ceramic and Review. Make three object cards with the same gray finish and liftable catalog records. The first record lists wood, the second lists ceramic and the third has a blank material field. Put aside a fourth card with a different finish and a record listing wood. For rule practice, tell the player the shipment is ready for sorting and give them the sorting rule. The facilitator asks for a placement and points to the relevant field after the decision, then lets the player revise. For an inquiry workshop, open with “These objects look similar. What could help us tell them apart?” Let learners choose what to compare, inspect a record, or ask for another sample. A help card can suggest comparing the material fields. Notice which evidence learners choose and whether a record changes a guess. End by asking what they would inspect next.

**Run a comparison for rule practice.** Use the same cards and rule to test a color cue on its own.

The curator’s rule in this fictional game is to sort by the base material listed on the catalog record and flag an item for review when that field is blank. The player fills a display cabinet. The wood bin is green, the ceramic bin is red and the review button is blue. In version A, object tags always match the correct destination’s color. The creator expects the colors to make sorting faster. In version B, the same tag colors vary independently of the correct destination. Both versions use the same labels, access options, practice cases, time allowance and feedback. Leave the catalog visible. Have learners predict where each object belongs before feedback explains how the catalog field decides it.

Take a short starting measure with comparable cases. After practice, give each learner new cases using the same rule, without hints or scores. Show two green-tagged items with catalog records listing wood and ceramic, then a red-tagged item with the base-material field blank. A correct response uses the material field for the first two and flags the third for review. Record the bin or flag, the evidence the learner identifies, and any assistance or failed input. Also record the practice win rate and whether the learner opts into another round.

| Possible observation | Decision |
|---|---|
| Version A fills cabinets faster, but learners follow green for both fresh items. Version B learners split the items and cite the catalog field more often from comparable starting scores. | Remove the correlation between tag and answer during practice. Keep readable labels and compare again with a new set of cases. |
| Both versions yield comparable correct fresh decisions and reasons, and version A improves independent navigation. | Keep the color aid for guided practice and retain an unprompted check. This result weakens the case for changing the colors. |
| Learners in both versions explain the base-material rule but drop items in neighboring bins. | Test a larger drop target or a click-to-select input with the same cases. If learners can submit their intended choices and still choose the wrong compartment, inspect how the bins are labeled. |
| Learners in both versions complete the practice set but assign items with missing records to a bin. | Test a practice case that makes “review” a viable action and explains when to use it. Check the next unseen missing-record case before changing pace or adding levels. |
