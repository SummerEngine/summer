# Test wayfinding by sight, sound and text

Start with a rule for the trip that the player can explain in their own terms. Give each cue a purpose. A destination signal can establish the bearing to a room. A doorway cue can identify the way in. A stable reference can help a player get their bearings after turning. Make a simple route that lets you observe each decision in order.

## Lay out the night conservatory

A runner takes a seed tray from a bench to the humidifier and comes back for a second tray. Set the humidifier just behind a glass partition so its clicks are audible from the bench. Put the nursery door at the far end of the glass. On the way back, an optional orchid room opens off the aisle.

| Route event | Cue to prototype | What the player can try |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At the bench | A slow, familiar click from the humidifier and an optional spoken destination name | Identify the destination before moving. |
| At the partition | Soft air at the nursery doorway, a pale door frame, and a route entry called Nursery Door | Follow the wall to the opening and enter it. |
| On the return | An occasional tap from a loose tool above the bench and the same Nursery Door name from either side | Reorient and give a direction to the bench. |
| At the orchid room | A separate name and a leaf-shaped handle with its own sound | Discover the room and decide when to return. |

Set the clips and the legend in a quiet sketch first. Add fans or music one source at a time. If the intended experience is stereo, test at the intended listening distance and playback setup. Provide a route description with stable room and exit names for players who cannot use directional audio. Let players replay the useful part at the junction. Try that route, and test the cue through the closed door, before adding more rooms.

## Find where the trip breaks

Start a first-time visitor at the bench. Ask them to deliver the tray, return and do it again. Note how they open the route view, when they first identify the nursery, the first doorway they try, any wall contacts, and the kind of help they need. Ask which clue made sense to them. Track the input and display or listening settings with each run.

| What you observe | One comparison |
| --- | --- |
| The visitor misses the air sound whenever a fan starts. | Lower the fan in a matched layout. Check whether the visitor can now identify the opening before walking. |
| The visitor knows the humidifier is ahead and keeps walking into glass. | Keep the humidifier where it is. Add a doorway cue and a path description, then check which one helps the visitor describe the connection. Test the two additions individually if you need to choose one. |
| The visitor describes the route correctly and turns the wrong way after changing their listening direction. | Compare movement tied to a fixed world direction with movement tied to facing. Use matched unfamiliar layouts and vary the order so each input scheme gets a first attempt. |
| The visitor finds the orchid room, explores, then returns unaided. | Ask how they found their way out and whether they can repeat the required delivery. Preserve a detour they chose and understood. |

Invite blind and low-vision players to help define the route task and choose cues they can use. Give people a way to get from launch through settings to the bench on the intended device. The journey includes that entry sequence. A successful trip after a facilitator sets everything up can leave a separate access problem to test.
