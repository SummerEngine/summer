# Multiplayer and social play

Use the creator's promise and the evidence already available. If called during an interview, continue from their latest answer with one relevant question and wait for their reply. Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## Invent an interaction

Start with something one person should do because of another person. If the pitch supplies only a setting, ask “What would you like one player to remember that another player did?” Name the desired exchange, such as making a favor someone can return. Identify each human player's interest and give one of them a choice over a shared object that can help or hinder the other. Make the cost of that choice readable and give the other player a chance to answer. Prototype those two moves before adding rules for chat, teams, or rewards.

In the invented paper game *Friday Frequency*, two community radio hosts each start with one fresh promo per night. The early slot reaches two listener groups and the late slot reaches one. The host setting the schedule can put their own promo first or give the early slot to the other host, giving up one group of listeners that night. Post the schedule with the scheduler's name and the reach of each promo. The other host takes over the schedule the next night. Play four nights with two people and no discussion during play. Track each favor in the first three nights and what its recipient does the following night. Ask each player privately which earlier move influenced their choice. Keep the schedule as a way to communicate if players recognize and answer a specific favor. If their reasons are unclear, replay the final night with the same reach totals and a new partner taking over the other show. Look for a choice that depends on what the previous partner did.

## Inspect the game

Follow one shared commitment from the first useful cue through its consequence. Locate the replay, rule, or recording. At the last instant each role could still change the outcome, record what that player could see, what they believed, what they could say or withhold, which actions the rules allowed, and who paid the cost. Include a quieter, eliminated, or returning role if the game promises them a way to participate. Distinguish a right to suggest a move, the power to commit it, and the opportunity to respond after it happens.

Ask each player privately what they thought was happening and what they wanted to do. Use the same moment and ask before a group debrief. Compare their replies with the inputs, cues and rules. A silent player might be waiting for their next action, withholding useful information on purpose, or giving up after a previous suggestion went unheard. A recording can establish that a warning played. The recipient's account can establish what they took it to mean. Mark missing viewpoints when only one replay or account is available.

If one role helps another, ask what the recipient can still decide after the help arrives. In the next round, ask a spectator privately whether they chose to watch and which action would let them take part. Test that action at a specific moment if the player wants to return and cannot find a way in.

## Work a decision

*Harbor Relay* is an invented prototype about a crew making risky towing calls together. The winch operator alone can commit to the east or west channel or call a hold that costs reserve rope. The selected route is visible to the crew. At second 18 the lookout sees the east channel closing, at second 21 they send a generic danger ping, and at second 24 the operator commits east. The crew finishes the tow with damaged cargo. These observations and replies illustrate the method.

| Role | View before commitment | Right and reported intent |
|---|---|---|
| Lookout | Forecast shows east will close. | Can send a generic ping. “I wanted west. I thought the ping said which channel.” |
| Winch operator | Sees both routes and receives the ping. | Can commit or hold. “I thought the ping meant the load was slipping. I would have waited if I'd understood.” |
| Deckhand | Sees the rope reserve and the proposed route. | Can release the rope during the tow and lose the load. “I kept quiet about the reserve. I wanted to watch the tension and act if it got worse.” |

Start with the mismatch between the lookout's message and the operator's interpretation. Repeat a matched fresh tow with a channel marker on the same ping. Keep timing, route options, information ownership and the costs of towing or waiting fixed. Record whether the crew uses or contests the advice before commitment. Ask the operator privately afterwards what they took the signal to mean. If an operator understands the warning and deliberately accepts the risk, ask whether the promise gives the lookout more influence. Test that separately by letting the lookout call the existing hold at the existing cost and deadline. Check whether the lookout uses it to protect a goal and whether the operator still discusses the call. Follow the deckhand through the next release opportunity and ask what they were watching for before changing their role.

A paper version can preserve the clock, send private forecast cards and use a token for commitment. Keep the reason to pass information and the consequences of using it. Run the paper comparison with the same interval between forecast and commitment. A trial that lets the crew confer at leisure can test whether they understand a marker. Use a clock to test whether it reaches the operator in time.

## Interview the creator

Start with one concrete decision if it is still unknown. “Before the operator chose east, what could the lookout do to change the call?” Use the creator's own roles and stakes. Each row gives a possible next question. Choose the first that can change the next test, ask it on its own, and wait for the reply.

| Latest answer | Next question |
|---|---|
| The player lacked a useful signal. | “What could they learn in time to make a different choice?” |
| The signal landed but meant something else to its recipient. | “What did the recipient think the signal was asking them to do?” |
| Both understood the risk and disagreed. | “Who is meant to have the final call when they disagree?” |
| The player understood and stayed quiet. | “What did that player say they were waiting for?” |
| The creator wants someone watching to take part again. | “What is the first decision a returning player should be able to affect?” |

If the creator has only a pitch, treat each answer about a player's future intent as a hypothesis. Stop the interview when one replay, rules check, or playtest can settle the open decision.

## Try the same method in competition

In a three-player salvage auction, the player in last place can use their final permit to strand either rival's shipment. First establish what they can still earn. A credible threat might win a concession. A player whose own contract is impossible might use the same threat to decide who wins. Replay matched late positions with the same starting bids, legal actions and deadlines. Change only the trailing contract's threshold so it becomes reachable with that permit. Rotate seats. If players with a newly reachable contract pursue it, the old position may have left too little worth doing for themselves. If they still make threats to secure deals the other players deliberately accept, the permit supports bargaining. Hear from each role before deciding whether to change its leverage.

A stalled auction calls for a different test. From an early position, record what each bidder keeps by waiting while the others spend permits. Offer one expiring contract that a bidder can fulfill immediately. Watch whether the player who was waiting now bids for a gain of their own.

## Resume after an interruption

In an asynchronous game, watch the person whose turn was pending when everyone left. Can they tell it is their turn, reconstruct their plan, and finish a legal move on their own? Test a visible turn marker on a saved board after a gap. If players now identify the mover and finish their turns, preserve the independent schedule. If they already know whose turn it is but ask what the team had agreed, compare the same position with the previous public commitment recorded. Keep hidden plans private if bluffing depends on them.

## Make the decision

Preserve uneven roles when each promised participant can identify their chance to influence play and can use it or choose to wait. Change the cue when players use the new information before commitment and the intended uncertainty survives. Change a commitment or re-entry rule when informed players repeatedly miss a chance they were promised and a single rule change lets them act. In *Harbor Relay*, cut a proposed unanimous confirmation if a separate comparison shows that it delays the tow and players merely echo the operator's call. Revisit the cut if the confirmation leads the crew to reconsider a route they would otherwise have taken.

For a genre-specific experiment, read the [matching design cases](../social.md). Return the observed commitment and its locator, the players' accounts or missing viewpoints, the verdict, and the one result that could reverse it. Return it to the creator or the calling skill in the same conversation.
