# Progression and replay

Use the creator's promise and the evidence already available. If called during an interview, continue from their latest answer with one relevant question and wait for their reply. Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## Inspect the game

Trace one actual encounter through the reward offer, equip or bank decision, reset if present, and the next encounter where the reward could matter. Record the build or rules version, the seed or checkpoint, available options, what the player could see, what they chose and when the effect first mattered. At a reset, list separately what happens to carried loot, banked currency, unlocked options and equipped power. A fresh inventory may still carry a familiar route or a learned response in the player's memory.

Determine what the reward is meant to do. A power increase can make the player feel stronger, an alternative build can change route or timing, and an unlock can be a satisfying endpoint. Use the smallest comparison below that could change the verdict.

## Invent a reward and its first use

When the creator asks for a design, start with a reachable situation where the player could wish for another action. Turn the wish into a rule. Specify when the action is legal, what it changes, what it costs, and whether ownership and uses survive a reset. Write the prerequisites before choosing where to place them. Then author a short sequence in which the player earns the reward, can try it once with little at stake, and can choose when or where to use it under a changed condition.

Produce a complete first pass with the initial state, legal actions and success condition. Trace one intended success and look for a cheaper success that skips the promised decision. Try the same reward in two states where you want the player to use it differently. Reject candidates that strand required steps or let one action serve every situation when adaptation is the goal. For a hand-playable example and a method for generating alternatives, use [Design a reward and its first use](references/design-a-reward.md).

## Interview the creator

If the creator has a premise and wants reward ideas, start with a moment they want the reward to change. When the desired action is already clear, [draft the first playable reward](references/design-a-reward.md). For an existing reward, ask for a before-and-after pair if needed. “Facing the same threat on the next run, what could the player decide differently?” Continue from their answer with one question and wait.

| If the creator says… | Ask next |
|---|---|
| “I know the loop, but need reward ideas.” | “What do you want the player to be able to do after the first success?” Use their answer to write a reward rule and a short sequence that puts it to use. |
| “You do more damage.” | “Which familiar fight should feel different once the player gets stronger?” If they expect a tactic to disappear or emerge, find the damage or timing threshold. If they want the same tactic to feel more powerful, compare it against the same opponent. |
| “Players can use a different build.” | “When would you take the old item over this one?” An answer tied to a threat gives you the second encounter for a loadout comparison. An answer tied only to rarity points toward availability or power. |
| “The new ability opens older areas.” | “What could a player do at a place they remember that was impossible on the first visit?” Trace that specific action and the reason to return. |
| “Players farm the first room to afford it.” | “What does that purchase let them do that they cannot do on the preceding attempt?” That identifies the first consequential purchase and the stretch to measure. |
| “It's about the nerve to leave with what you've got.” | “At what exit, with what in their bag, would you expect a cautious player to turn back?” If the answer depends on facts the player cannot see, test the cue before changing the payout. |
| “Easy wins teach the mechanic.” | “What should the player be able to do in an unfamiliar room with the starting kit?” Use the action they name for a bypass and transfer check. |
| “They got the final reward and stopped.” | “What did you expect them to want to do once they had it?” If the reward completes their goal, ask about satisfaction before prescribing another loop. |

If the creator is unsure, offer one short contrast using the current game, such as returning with a stronger shot versus returning with an ability that changes where they stand. Use their choice to select the experiment. End the interview once a decision or test is more useful than another question.

## Test whether two builds change a decision

Replay two available builds from the same checkpoint with the same base kit, enemy behavior and starting resources. Find an encounter where the first build ought to matter and a second where the other should matter. Write down the cheapest successful sequence and which action changes between them. A new attack animation may leave the player's plan intact. A damage increase can create a new plan if it crosses a threshold such as defeating a threat before its first attack. Show a preview of the existing effect in one comparison if the modeled tradeoff is real but the option goes unused. Ask the player to predict when it applies before replaying.

**Worked example from invented rules.** In a salvage game, a diver chooses a pump or a cutter at equal cost and leaves the dock with 6 battery. A seed offers a flooded passage, a sealed hatch, or a choice between them. The diver takes one route. Its direct path and bypass lead to the same 10-credit haul and exit. Operating the exit costs 2 battery after crossing.

| Fixed encounter | Pump | Cutter |
|---|---|---|
| Flooded passage with a dry bypass | Pump through for 1 and reach the exit with 5. | Take the dry bypass for 4 and reach the exit with 2. |
| Sealed hatch with a hatch-free bypass | Take the bypass for 4 and reach the exit with 2. | Cut through for 1 and reach the exit with 5. |

Both builds can clear either encounter. On a direct path, a diver can spend 3 battery to collect a sample in an alcove beside the exit and still have 2 to leave. Each sample counts toward an optional survey that requires three. The other tool supports the bypass and exit alone. Remove the alcove and both still earn 10 credits. If the objective is to bank those credits and crossing takes the same time, the extra battery has no use in this slice. A preference for a particular route is still possible and needs a player's account.

If the creator reports that everyone picks the pump, compare exposure before adding a tool. Sample saved seeds using the actual generator settings. Tally whether each tool permits the extra sample, how much battery remains, and whether a player can learn the branch type before choosing a tool. Suppose a paper seed set contains 20 flood-only, 3 hatch-only and 1 seed with both routes visible at the loadout. The pump can reach the sample on 21 of 24 seeds and the cutter on 4 of 24. This is an imbalance in opportunity in that set, regardless of how different the rooms look.

Replay the same hatch seed with the existing branch type visible at the dock. A switch to the cutter when the player can predict the benefit supports tuning the cue. Continued pump use with correct predictions may be rational if changing kit costs more later, or if the player values only safe returns. Swap the branch type in one fixed seed with reward, risk and kit costs held constant to test whether a change in opportunity is enough. If the cutter also drops randomly, count how often players actually see it while they can equip it before judging how often they choose it. Keep both tools if each supports a reason players value. Tune exposure if one opportunity is rare by accident. Merge or cut a tool if removing it preserves valued plans and the intended fantasy. A deliberately stable training route can make even a narrow distribution useful.

## Test what an unlock changes in a familiar place

Write the required order of actions and the room connections separately. Follow the first visit to a visible obstacle, acquisition of the ability, and the earliest legal return. Record what the player saw before, what now works, and what the player could choose after using it.

**Invented two-visit example.** At the old dock, a fallen gangway with painted lifting rings blocks access to a repair pier. The winch cannot lift it. Later, the player unlocks a portable crane and uses it on lifting rings at the workshop. Back at the dock, lifting the gangway opens a choice between repairing boats where they are moored and towing them to the yard.

First try the winch and check that returning with the crane actually opens those choices. At acquisition, ask the player where else the crane could work. In a comparison, repeat the dock's painted ring markings at the workshop with the return route and repair options fixed. If players now remember the gangway and use the pier, keep the revisit and tune the cue. If they predict what the pier offers but skip it, offer a short return with those options unchanged. Uptake then supports reducing travel. If they still see no reason to choose the pier, give it a distinct job or remove the detour. Use [exploration and puzzles](../exploration/guide.md) when players want the destination and struggle to find it.

## Check when spending pays back

Write a small ledger across the first few attempts. For each real purchase record the cost, time or attempts before it unlocks a useful action, payout and losses on the next attempt, and how that outcome funds another purchase. Compare a safe repeat route with an advancing route at the same starting power. A reinforcing loop occurs when an upgrade increases the income that buys further upgrades. A flat total after ten runs can hide an early purchase that determines all subsequent choices.

For an invented shop example, spend a starting 12 credits on either a winch or a hull patch. A safe return yields 4 credits. On that route the winch adds 4 per return and the patch only helps on deep dives. A second upgrade costs 12. Before a second purchase, the winch route reaches balances of 8 and 16 after two returns. The patch route reaches 4, 8 and 12 after three. On two comparable deep dives, check whether the patch prevents enough losses or enables a useful route that recovers the difference. If shallow repetition is crowding out deep dives, tune the winch bonus alone. A bonus of 2 still buys the next upgrade in two safe returns. A bonus of 1 takes three. Test whether players now choose when to make a deep dive. If players still farm, a risky route with unreadable threats or poor rewards is a stronger explanation than the winch alone. Keep the quick return if the shallow route offers the planning or recovery the creator wants.

Measure cadence at the scale where players run out of a reason to continue. Count time from spawn to a useful decision, from that decision to a spend, and from a spend to the first new use. A sequence of drops between identical purchases can leave that whole interval unchanged. If the creator wants optional mastery after the final reward, offer a replay with the reward already earned and see whether players choose and try a different approach. If they describe a satisfying endpoint and leave, keep the ending. Ask about the endpoint before interpreting the session count.

## Find where leaving becomes worthwhile

At a real exit, trace what the player can bank now, what success in the next room adds, what failure retains, and where the next exit is. Ask the player to explain their estimate before choosing. Include the objective, time and any loss of future access if those matter.

**Invented one-room comparison.** A diver at the exit can bank 24 credits now or take one more room for 12 extra. Success ends the run and banks 36. Failure banks 0 and leaves equipment and the previous bank intact. Assume the player values expected credits for this decision, with no time or restart cost in the calculation. Continuing pays when its chance of success exceeds `24 / 36`, or two thirds. At 75% the room returns 27 credits in expectation. At 50% it returns 18. With only 6 carried, even a 50% chance returns 9 against 6 now. These odds are inputs to a paper comparison. In a real game estimate them for the route and proficiency under review. A player may value certainty, a collection goal, or an experiment with the new tool.

If players always stay, test whether they know what they would lose. Reveal the at-risk total with costs and payouts fixed. If they predict it correctly and still stay, check whether they reliably survive, value the extra room on its own, or require its loot to progress. If a high survival chance or mandatory loot removes the intended dilemma, change that rule alone and compare choices with low and high amounts carried. If players always leave, trace recovery after failure and the time to make another consequential choice. A replay of solved rooms can be the main cost even when little loot is lost. Preserve the current incentives when the aim is a brisk victory route or a deliberately safe exit after a long session.

## Test the player's skill at equal power

Name the target response in play, the cue that should prompt it, and a visible success criterion. From the same checkpoint try credible bypasses, such as upgraded damage, healing through the threat, or an ally doing the work. Mark which clears actually require the response. A single successful bypass disproves a claim of necessity.

For a mastery claim, compare players with the starting kit and players given the carryover benefit on the familiar encounter. Then give both groups the same starting kit and an unfamiliar equivalent. For example, a damage boost can destroy a guard before its sweep. In the follow-up, use a different room and guard with the same sweep cue and score whether the player takes cover before it lands, then uses the recovery window. Count the opportunities each first encounter actually required and observe whether failures come from anticipating the wrong move, missing the cue or failing to execute a correct response. If the boosted group clears more often initially and misses the response at equal power, test moving the boost after the first required practice. If both groups transfer the response, the boost may be compatible with mastery. A returning player who already knows the cue may welcome the shortcut.

## Make the decision

Return the verdict to the creator or calling skill with the inspected decision, its locator, the strongest rival explanation and one result that would change the verdict. State whether the evidence came from a rule trace, modeled outcome, observed play or the player's own account. Keep the reward when it delivers the promised power, expression or closure. Tune the variable implicated by the comparison, such as timing, option visibility, price, exposure or recovery cost. Cut a layer when a comparison without it preserves the decisions and experience the creator values. Add a reward when there is a desired action or milestone the current game cannot support and a small prototype makes it possible. For a genre-specific experiment, read the [matching design cases](../systems.md).
