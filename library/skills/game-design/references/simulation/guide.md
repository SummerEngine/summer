# Simulation and economies

Use the creator's promise and the evidence already available. If called during an interview, continue from their latest answer with one relevant question and wait for their reply. Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## Inspect the game

Pick one goal a player values. Trace the smallest chain that makes it possible, from a source through a stock, a conversion or commitment, and a use the player cares about. Use the actual save, rules, or recording. Write down the units, incoming and outgoing amounts, the order of events, and the time from an action to its effect. Include any cap, batch requirement, shared work slot, or competing use that can change the outcome. Take a snapshot before the first consequential choice and another after the loss or investment. For each, record what the player could see, which actions were legal, and the last time each action could still affect the goal.

Distinguish a transfer from a rule that changes a future flow. Spending fuel on a firing uses up stock. Buying production capacity with a firing's proceeds can make the next purchase easier. Follow the second purchase as well as the first. Check when the investment starts paying back, what it displaces before then, and whether a cap or escalating cost changes its value. Compare two plausible plans from the same opening. A script or paper trace can establish when a plan is feasible. Observe play to learn which plan players perceive and choose.

In a sandbox or cozy game, start with something the player chose to do. Follow a day spent on that project, a day spent on upkeep, and a return after skipping upkeep. Observe what players actually postpone, whether they can resume their project, and whether they value the time they spend waiting or tending. A quiet interval can be the time a player uses to decorate, plan, or wander. Give that interval the same attention as a shortage.

If a system changes while the player is away, compare two copies of the same save. For example, let three shifts pass at a staffed mill, once with a player present and once while they visit town. Keep orders, inputs, storage and staffing fixed. On return, compare grain consumed, flour delivered and the pending order. If the results differ, find the first shift where they diverged. If the game promises reduced output during an absence, check whether players knew that before leaving and could plan for it.

## Invent an economy from one action

When the creator has a setting and a blank rules sheet, name the experience they want a decision to create. Then list two things a person in that setting could directly change. For each, finish the sentence “I changed ___, so later I could ___ and had to give up ___.” Sketch the action whose consequences express the promise with the fewest separate commands.

Invent a canal garden where the intended pleasure is deciding what one morning's water can make possible. Draw a tank, seed trays and a floating footbridge. At dawn one gate sends the tank's 2 water tokens down either channel. East waters the trays and makes two seedlings ready for collection by noon. West lifts the footbridge to bank level so the player can collect parts from the repair shed in one walking turn. With the bridge low, the towpath takes two turns. The player has one walking turn before the day's deliveries close. Collecting the seedlings from watered trays also takes one turn.

Place a seedling request on the map and work through both gate positions. Then replace it with a repair-parts request and work through both again. East fills the first request and west fills the second. Now offer both together. A neighbour wants seedlings before today's market, and a broken cart will slow tomorrow's deliveries until it gets a part. Let the player choose what to protect and carry the unfilled request into the next day. If one choice always wins, look for a day when the other loss matters. To develop a hybrid, carry the changed canal map into the afternoon walking segment so the raised bridge is a route the player can use. Keep the canal as a separate activity if its decisions shape a valued task or serve a distinct pleasure.

Draw the water reaching its destination before showing the wet trays or the bridge rising. Ask the player to point to what changed while stepping through the paper scene. Ask them to predict which route opens before moving the bridge.

If the simulation makes a claim about how a society works, try two rules that disagree. In a fictional weaving town, a technique could spread when ten workshops are active or when a master trains an apprentice. Try nine workshops with one completed apprenticeship, then ten workshops where nobody has taught the technique. The two rules give opposite results in each town. One invites the player to expand production, the other to make room for teaching. Choose the actions that express the creator's premise and identify any real-world claim that needs evidence.

## Worked example with a town kiln

The player starts with a glaze order for the fair and would also like to paint their stall sign. On Day 1 they have 5 briquettes and 3 coins. The supplier deposits 2 briquettes before the Day 1 dusk firing, then makes no routine deliveries on Days 2 and 3. At dusk on each of those three days, the order requires a full batch of 3 briquettes. A missed firing consumes zero fuel and closes this fair order. Ordinary jobs open when the supplier returns on Day 4, whichever way the order ends.

Every morning the player can use one work slot to paint the sign, book a shipment, or leave the slot free. Painting completes the sign. A shipment costs 3 coins and delivers 3 briquettes the following dawn, even during the routine closure. This is the only extra fuel source during the closure and the player can afford one shipment.

| Time | Recoverable branch | Unrecoverable branch for the fair order |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 dusk | The player leaves the morning free. Routine delivery and firing leave `5 + 2 - 3 = 4` briquettes. | Same opening, with 4 briquettes after firing. |
| Day 2 morning and dusk | Books a shipment for all 3 coins. Firing leaves `4 - 3 = 1` briquette, with 3 in transit. | Paints the sign. Firing leaves `4 - 3 = 1` briquette and 3 coins on hand. |
| Day 3 dawn and dusk | Receives the shipment and paints the sign. The final firing succeeds and leaves `1 + 3 - 3 = 1` briquette. | Can book a shipment that arrives on Day 4. At dusk, the 1 briquette is two short of a full firing. The fair order fails and the briquette stays in stock. |

The last booking opportunity is Day 2 morning, when stock still covers that evening's firing. Day 2 dusk is too late even with 3 coins. Booking on Day 1 is also viable. Advance planning commits the only cash reserve and occupies a morning. Painting on Day 3 still completes the sign. Watch whether players value putting it up a day earlier or keeping a morning free. If every informed player makes the same booking and the time conflict carries no felt cost, test whether a booking needs to use the work slot.

## Make the decision

Suppose a tester says, “I ran out of fuel and still had the money.” Before changing the economy, ask them to reconstruct what they expected at the moment they committed the work slot. Compare from the same saved state or paper timeline and change one variable at a time.

| What the player does or can do | Smallest comparison and decision |
|---|---|
| Books too late because the current stock looks safe, or expects instant delivery. | At Day 2 morning, show the existing closure, the next arrival, and the forecast shortage at Day 3 firing. Preserve costs and rates. If players can now predict the shortage and act or accept the loss, preserve the timing and improve the forecast. If they already predict it, look at their work-slot options. |
| Understands the shortage but reaches the decision after the last legal booking, for example because the game introduces shipping on Day 3. | From the same Day 3 save, test delivery before that day's firing at the same price. If players regain a meaningful choice at that point and value the fair order, consider opening a recovery window or introducing the existing action before the cutoff. If the creator wants a firm failure, check that players had an informed earlier commitment and can still pursue a valued goal afterward. |
| Deliberately paints the sign, then reloads because they think the failed order will close the workshop. | In one version, show the existing Day 4 job opening alongside the fair order on Day 1. Keep the contract and all payoffs fixed. If players knowingly let the fair go, finish the sign and take an ordinary job, preserve the choice and clarify what a failure costs. If they already know the workshop stays open and still regret losing the fair, ask what they wanted from that specific order. |
| Repeats a routine confirmation on every successful firing and makes the same decisions regardless of it. | In a paper pass, resolve the confirmation automatically with the same fuel cost, timing and chance to interrupt. Cut the prompt if players still read the kiln state, make the same meaningful choices and welcome the extra time. Preserve the ritual if players seek it out or use it to notice and act on a changing condition. |

Give the player the same goal and starting state in each comparison. Record the expected arrival or shortfall before commitment, the action taken, its result at the next relevant event, and what the player does after success or loss. Ask why they chose that action before explaining the rules. If a timely order cleared payment and the fuel never arrived, check the delivery event against the recorded rules before changing the schedule. Check that a proposed recovery restores an actual route and that players can see and use it. A solved timeline alone establishes feasibility.

## Interview the creator

Use the creator's last answer and ask just the first unanswered question that could change the test. Wait for the reply.

- If the player goal is unclear, ask “What would the player choose to spend a free morning on?”
- If they name the activity and the constraint is unclear, ask “What changes tomorrow if they spend the whole morning on it?”
- If they describe an unwanted loss, ask “What could the player see before their last chance to avoid that loss?”
- If the player could see the loss, ask “Which action could still arrive in time?”
- If the stated promise is unhurried expression and the player understands the tradeoff, ask “Which task should the player be able to leave undone and still enjoy the next day?”

If an answer already establishes the problem, test it or return the decision. When the creator is unsure whether a setback should last, describe one playable day after a hard loss and one after a costly recovery. Ask which day better serves their intended experience.

## Diagnose other economy problems

- Start from one save and run the same economy twice. Spend the first surplus on more production in one run and on a different goal in the other. Record the rounds when the investment first earns back its price and makes a second purchase possible. Replay with a different first purchase date if both plans converge before the next decision. Compare those results with what players do from that save.
- If a shared cost seems to hit the wrong player, trace who triggers it and who pays at the next use. At a print fair, the third batch uses an extra sheet from the booth that prints it. One booth has two batches and the other has one. Have each booth print all its batches before the other starts. Swap which prints first. If the cost always follows the last booth, inspect scheduling. If the cost is meant to reflect total press use, try charging for the second batch from a booth and replay both orders.
- When players repeat an unwanted action, record each reward it grants. In a greenhouse trial, watering a full tray may earn practice credit while producing no more seedlings. Ask growers what they came to earn. If they want practice, compare giving it for growing a tray to harvest with giving it for each watering. Check what players do once all ready trays are tended.
- Write a day around a goal a player chose and another after they skip an assigned task. Give both days a reachable next action. If a birdwatcher skips the town errands to follow a flock, check whether they can still reach a second sighting the next day. If a missed errand locks the trail, test that dependency before adding a new bird to find.

For a genre-specific experiment, read the [matching design cases](../systems.md). Return the observed moment and its locator, the first constraint that affected the goal, the strongest rival cause, and a preserve, change, or cut verdict. Name the result that would reverse the verdict and what still needs a playtest.
