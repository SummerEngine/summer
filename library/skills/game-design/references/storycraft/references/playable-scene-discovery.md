# Playable scene discovery

Use this workshop when a story depends on what the player notices, what they do while an event is pending, or what a familiar place means when they return. You can try it with a paper map, a few objects and a person performing the responses.

## Give the place a routine the player can learn

Find one route a person actually takes through the setting. What do they carry? Where must they slow down? What can someone hear before they arrive? In a library that lends instruments, a cello case scrapes against the stair rail unless its carrier stops to turn it. Two details can sustain that trip. Compress the routine stretches while keeping the bend intelligible. Give the player a reason to travel in both directions. When the cello case is gone on the way back and the peg that held it is still moving, someone has been there in the interval.

Underline one concrete noun in a scene draft. Write what a curious player might do with it, how the world responds and what that tells them. A rolling staircase with a red brake invites someone to test the stop. If its job is to show why high shelves are accessible, let the brake click and keep the staircase steady. If the brake has a role later, make that same click and the steady footing reliable evidence in both scenes. Give ordinary traces of use room to make a place credible. Prominence and placement shape which objects players expect to use.

## Decide where the author sets the terms and where the player acts

Write three sentences. **What has already happened? What can the player decide now? What will someone else do about it?** Establish the first as briefly as the situation allows. Give the second an action and work out what happens if the player declines. Write a response for each meaningful outcome. When the scene requires an established fact, place it before the first opportunity to contradict it. Carry the player's decision through the reaction.

Try handing over control at the first sentence in which a player can form an intention. Ask what they would like to do. If they wait to be told, give the errand a person, obligation or pleasure worth attending to now. An authored character can have a clear limit. If the librarian refuses to unlock a cabinet that holds another person's unclaimed belongings, give the player her reason early and a credible way to ask the owner. Repeatedly interrupting the player to deliver a fixed sequence can make the invitation to act feel hollow.

## Let discovery change with the route

List what the player must be able to do separately from what they need to know. To record a musician from the hall, the player needs access to the booth and a working microphone. To understand why the musician leaves a beat empty, they might hear the first verse or notice that a second score is still open on its stand. Use plain sentences for the facts and mark which later moment needs each one. Play both orders and write responses using only the evidence available on that route.

An open middle gives a stuck player somewhere else to turn. It can also let accomplishments feel isolated. In a narrower scene each obstacle tells the player what matters next. Give the player a clear point where paths converge when a shared outcome gains meaning from their earlier discoveries. Keep essential evidence available on the near side of a visible commitment, or give the player a recovery route. If both orders support the same inference, let the player discover either first.

## Give time and repetition a dramatic purpose

Decide what advances the scene's clock and what becomes possible or impossible while it runs. For an appointment, give the player a cue, a reason to stay and a way to learn what happened if they left. For an emergency, establish when delay changes the outcome and give the player time to recognize that change. For a quiet encounter, let the player decide when to leave.

Draft three visits to one location. On the first, supply orientation and one observation needed for a later inference. On the second, let a consequence of the player's action occupy the first line. On the third, keep the practical details and shorten familiar description. Let the line that makes a room funny land when it is fresh. Keep a necessary cue reproducible until the player can act on it.

## Worked scene — Which side of the curtain?

The town theatre has closed. Its painted harbour curtain is going on display in the library. You used to sell tickets. The old usher has asked you to set out the opening-day chairs on one side of the freestanding curtain. She keeps the library doors closed until you give the word. Visitors will be able to walk around it afterwards. Your decision determines what the gathered audience will see first.

**First playable moment.** A strip of sunlight lands where the front row might go. One chair is already there, set down facing the harbour. Across the room the usher brings in another stack. The player can sit in the chair, carry it around the curtain, turn on a work lamp or ask the usher who put the first chair there. She did. As a child she waited for the sight of that painted harbour every Saturday. She’ll set out the rest wherever you put the first chair. The fixed closure belongs in the opening. Choosing where to seat the town belongs in play.

**Two discoveries in either order.** Walk around the curtain and tilt the work lamp across a pale patch. Names appear in stitches along the backs of the seams. Inspect a mending ledger on the usher’s table to find the same names beside dates, hours and widths of cloth. It lists repeated repairs by a wardrobe crew across forty years. The stitches show the names sewn into the cloth. The ledger supplies their connection to the wardrobe crew and the dates of the repairs.

| Order played | What the player can infer | Next authored response |
|---|---|---|
| Stitches, then ledger | Several people signed individual seams. The dates then establish how long they kept the curtain in use. | Opening the ledger lets the player match the first stitched surname to an entry for a winter repair. |
| Ledger, then stitches | A crew repaired the same object over many seasons. Their names on the cloth show that they left a record the public never saw. | Moving the lamp gives the first clearly legible seam the surname the player just encountered on paper. |
| Chairs first, with either discovery still available | The painted face already matters to someone. The player can make an informed choice about that memory even if they never identify a maker. | The usher asks which side should greet the audience and waits for the player’s assent. The last chance to cross the room occurs before she opens the doors. |

**Use a return visit.** On the first walk behind the curtain, write the patch as three ridges casting hairline shadows. After the lamp reveals the stitches, keep them visible whenever the lamp still shines there. On returning from the ledger, give the player a new act, tracing the recurring surname from one repair into another. The usher can bring more chairs during the search. She waits for a signal before admitting anyone, whether the player reads for ten seconds or ten minutes. Hearing the stacked chairs settle is a sign of her work. It carries no hidden countdown.

**End with a consequence the player composed.** If the first row faces the painting, the usher sits down among her neighbours and points to the boat she used to watch from the cheap seats. If it faces the seams, the visitors lean in to read the nearest name. A child asks which end to start at. If the player has already matched the ledger, they can open it to the first repair and hand it along the row. Either group can walk to the other side afterwards. The chair from the opening is now occupied. The short coda resolves what the audience saw first and leaves them with plenty still to see.

**Try the scene on paper.** Use a folded card for the curtain, put three names beside drawn seams on its reverse, and enter the same names with dates and work hours on a second card. Give one reader the ledger first and another the stitched side first. After their second discovery, ask who made the marks and what lets them say so. Watch where each places the first row before asking why. A reader who found both clues and still thinks the names are a cast list has uncovered an attribution problem. Add a specific mending job beside one ledger entry. A reader who understands the repairs and chooses the harbour to honour the usher has produced a coherent ending. Preserve it.

## Check a scene that has stalled

- **The player waited somewhere else.** Ask what sign led them to expect the appointment there. Carry an audible cue into the adjoining space and try the two routes again with fresh players. A recording of a successful route shows that the meeting can happen. A first-time player's account tells you whether they had a reason to be there.
- **The player completed the action by accident.** Ask what they think caused the change. If a stagehand opens the door because the player is carrying a marked crate, show their glance at the mark and their decision while the crate is still visible.
- **A plausible attempt gets the same response everywhere.** Test a recurring line in the moment when it would be most revealing. “Put it over there” might suit an ordinary stack of crates. If one was the stagehand's toolbox, she might recognize a sticker before reaching for it. Let people answer from what they know and care about in that state.
- **The help panel gives away the next scene.** Offer help for something the player has encountered. Point to an observable detail first, then explain the inference. Give the exact action on request. Test each step with the obstacle in its actual state.
