# Viewpoint and reveals

Use a beat the creator already cares about. Write the events as they happened, then mark when each reader, player and character can learn about them. A timeline is the author's working record. Give the audience their own evidence inside the work.

## Change what the audience can infer

Show someone putting an extra plate back in the cupboard and a reader may imagine a guest who failed to arrive. Give them another detail if it matters which guest. A missing dinner guest carries different weight at the start of a mystery and at the end of a courtship. When the first reading misses, check what the scene has taught its audience to expect before sharpening the clue.

A sister writing to her brother can say “our usual place.” He knows which place. A stranger reading the letter may need the chipped cup or the whistle of a train to locate it. In a game, the two siblings can read the same map differently because one remembers how the room was used. Decide what the player needs to understand the immediate invitation and what they can discover later.

Try the same event three ways. In a story about a shared greenhouse, a worker slips a new name into the sign-up ledger overnight.

| What the audience encounters first | Question it invites | Next evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The worker adds the name and leaves a key | Will the morning crew let that person stay? | The crew starts turning out lights as the guest arrives. |
| The morning crew finds a new name in the ledger | Who let someone in, and why? | A sleeve left to dry by the heater has the worker's distinctive repair. |
| Two members of the crew tell the story afterwards | What did each consider a welcome? | One recalls the key, the other remembers being given a bench. Give both accounts a concrete action to describe. |

Move the information earlier or later. Spend longer where the audience can change its mind. Tell a pattern once or revisit a single event from someone else's position. “Every Sunday for six years, he set out that cup” gathers years into a habit. Spend three sentences on the Sunday he puts it back. To distinguish suspense from surprise, pause a fresh reader before the turn and ask what they predict and which detail led them there.

A turn can come through a dispute, a growing ability, or a discovery. End a quiet scene when a person can finally make a mark, receive a kindness or see what an ordinary gesture cost. An unresolved relationship can still leave the reader with a precise new question.

## Give a place and its people a particular use

Topography tells us where things stand. Atmosphere tells us what being there feels like to someone. If the kitchen table touches the door when it opens, a person carrying a cake must turn sideways. After a funeral, that person may turn sideways out of habit in a kitchen where the table is already gone. Move the encounter to another room. What stops making sense? Give an ordinary object enough room to make the setting feel lived in. If readers expect it to pay off, check how much attention the prose asks them to spend on it.

A central character, a complex character and a character who changes are three different decisions. Give someone at the edge of the action a private reason to attend. A cashier who wants to go home can still pull up a stool for a stranded customer. Try letting her interrupt the main exchange to slide the stool across. If the scene works without the cashier, keep her when that small act is part of the world the scene wants the reader to know. A complex person can hold the same conviction through a whole story.

“We’ve got room” while taking a coat off an empty chair makes a different offer from the same words while leaving the coat there. A narrator can also establish someone's impatience in four words. Choose quoted speech when the exact offer matters and reported speech when a whole exchange can pass quickly. Free indirect style keeps the grammar of third-person narration while borrowing a character's phrasing. “Mara shifted the coat. Surely he could sit down for one minute.” Give the guest a reason to hurry that Mara would never guess.

## Choose voice, perspective and trust separately

Identify who tells the story, the grammatical person, whose perspective selects the details, and what the teller knows at the time of telling. “I heard the front door click” can be a trustworthy account from someone who has no idea who closed it. Someone who says “We all agreed” and quotes only people allowed to stay in the room gives the reader a reason to question the account. If a scene includes another teller recounting an event, write whom they address and what they need from that listener. An eight-year-old hearing it tonight and an adult retelling it years later can give the same sentence different weight.

Read the sentence with the most conspicuous rhythm aloud, then try a plainer one. Listen for the effect that disappears. A greenhouse hinge that “cleared its throat” can make an empty room feel occupied. Repeating that sound just before a conversation can give it a second job. For translation or a performance, note whether an unusual phrase signals familiarity, distance or hesitation so the relationship survives a change in wording.

Ask for a reading and the visible or audible details that support it. A synopsis tells you what happened. A theme is an interpretation the presentation can support. Repeated details can carry that interpretation, and readers may sustain different judgments using the same evidence. To examine who has power in a scene, look at who gets a name, whose judgments pass as ordinary, and whose account can change the ending.

## Revise one beat — the dropped coin

**Shared facts.** In a stage trick, a borrowed coin appears under a glass. During the trick a second coin rolls across the stage. The assistant pockets it in full view. The drop is rehearsed. She needs the audience looking at her until the magician puts the glass down.

**From a child in the front row.** “The coin got away. It came straight for my shoe. The lady stamped on it, picked it up and winked at me. I winked back. Nobody else had to know.”

**From the assistant.** “Mina waited for the boy in the front row to look up. One wink. Two hundred faces turned to see who it was for. From the table behind her came the little tap of glass on wood. Now she could take her bow.”

The child's account invites us into a rescue. Mina's account lets us enjoy her control of a room. Both can end when the glass lifts and the volunteer recognizes the nick on their own coin. If the reader shares the child's belief, the ending can overturn it. If the reader understands the plan, the suspense comes from waiting for the tap. Changing the pronoun in either passage would leave that difference in knowledge intact.

For a playable version, cast the player as the editor of a recorded performance. The cutting desk holds a front-row shot of the drop and a rehearsal sheet with Mina's cue to hold the audience until the glass is set. Let either open first. Then let the player cut from Mina to the boy before or after the tap. The supplied evidence stays the same. The chosen instant changes whether the viewer can enjoy the boy's conviction or see him become part of the trick. Ask someone watching the cut when they first thought the drop was deliberate and which detail led them there. If a close-up of the second coin gives the trick away in both versions, try keeping its face out of frame until the reveal.
