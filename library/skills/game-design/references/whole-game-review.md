# Should this game gain a feature?

Use this when a creator asks for a whole-game review or proposes a feature whose purpose is still unclear. The worked examples below are fictional. Each shows which observations would change the recommendation.

## Follow one decision through the game

Find the first time the game lets the player do the thing it promises. In a detective game, this might be the first time two clues support different accounts. In a shop game, it might be the first time accepting one order costs the player another. Record what a newcomer has seen by then. A designer’s debug map, a later tutorial and a clue on an unvisited route each give the reviewer knowledge the player may lack.

Follow that decision into a later situation. What makes the same action worth doing differently? Trace the consequence into the next attempt or the end of the session. For a shop game, compare the first morning after the player fills the display they chose with a morning when they still have an unfilled shelf. Ask which moment they quit at, what they considered finished and what they would change on another day. A satisfying finish can justify keeping the ending even when few players start again.

Record a location or timestamp alongside each observation. A rule that permits a choice, a player making it and a player explaining why are three different pieces of evidence. Use the one that can answer the question. An inspection can show that two upgrades call the same function with the same values. It takes a player’s account to establish whether their different appearances give the choice meaning.

## Worked review of a cooperative delivery game

The creator’s promise is “two dockhands invent a delivery route together.” They ask whether to add a live map. In this example, either dockhand can propose a boat during loading. The pilot confirms the choice and departure locks the cargo list. One route is short but closes at high tide. Another stays open and takes longer. These facts can be verified in the rules.

**Inspect the first departure.** Locate the cargo list, the first visible tide signal, the last point where either player can change the boat, and the first event that tells the pair they chose well or badly. If the first tide signal appears after departure, the game can test adaptation on the water. The crew needs an earlier signal to use the tide in its boat choice. Compare showing the existing signal just before commitment with showing it at departure. Leave the chart fixed and ask what each player was able to use in their choice.

**Identify the open question.** Suppose the tide signal does appear in time. A recording shows the pilot selecting a boat while the loader stands at the dock. That alone leaves several plausible accounts. The loader might know a better route and be unable to signal it. They might enjoy delegating the decision. Or both players might believe the same boat wins under every tide. Ask each person privately what they expected the other could do, and what they would have chosen just before departure. Use their answers to choose a single comparison.

| If the pair reports this | Change one thing | What would change the diagnosis? |
|---|---|---|
| Both misread which inlet closes. Once told, both deliberately take the longer route. | Move the existing tide mark to the departure board. Keep timings and payouts fixed. | If both can now predict the closure but always choose the short route, compare the actual loss for arriving late with the time saved. The incentive may dominate the information. |
| The loader can explain the better route and wants to propose it. The pilot commits before the loader’s signal arrives. | Allow one cargo-side route suggestion before departure. Observe whether the pilot pauses or changes course when the suggestion differs. | A delivered suggestion and a reasoned disagreement support keeping the pilot’s authority. If the loader still needs continuous positional information during the voyage, a shared map earns a separate trial. |
| Both players understand the map, trade roles over successive trips, and describe that division as the pleasure of the game. | Keep the division. Examine a later delivery where both cargos compete for the same short window. | If one person’s choices cease to affect any delivery across roles, revisit who can influence the schedule. A quiet stretch on its own gives little reason to expand the interface. |

Run a paper version with a port diagram and two boat cards. Let one person see the cargo deadlines and the other see the tide. Use the game's communication rule and commitment timing. A solo participant holding both cards can establish that the route arithmetic works. Two participants can show what it costs to exchange information. If the pilot has five seconds to select the boat and the loader's signal arrives only after loading, keep both constraints in the prototype. Compare moving the signal earlier with giving the crew a longer deadline in separate trials.

Follow the [multiplayer specialist](multiplayer/guide.md) if the unresolved issue is how one person influences the other. Follow the [exploration specialist](exploration/guide.md) if both people lack the same route inference. Tell the creator which result would make a live map worth prototyping.

## Worked interview when there is only an idea

The creator says, “I’m making a cozy night train. Does it need crafting?” Ask one question and wait. “Imagine the train pauses for five minutes. What would you like the player to spend that time doing?” The next question depends on the answer.

- **“I want to learn what my passengers miss about home.”** Ask for one passenger request and two responses the passenger would read differently. If gathering ingredients would simply delay that exchange, try two conversations with the same item and a different choice of recipient. If the creator wants how the gift was made to communicate care, compare a ready-made gift with one meaningful choice in its preparation. Follow the [narrative specialist](narrative/guide.md).
- **“I want to decide what to use now and what to save for the next stop.”** Ask what the player knows about that stop when committing. Give them two supplies, two stops and one shared ingredient. If one allocation always serves both requests, change a deadline or capacity on paper and watch whether an interesting trade appears. A recipe catalog can wait until a single recipe earns its place. Follow the [simulation specialist](simulation/guide.md).
- **“I just like the thought of making tea while the scenery passes.”** Ask which motion or sound makes those seconds worth repeating. Offer one cup, an uninterrupted view and the option to sit through the stop. Observe whether a player makes a second cup when it earns nothing. Then ask whether the ritual gave them the pause they wanted.

End when the creator can name the experience they want and a small test can resolve the remaining uncertainty. State what would earn the proposed feature a place and what would make the simpler version worth keeping.
