# Test consequences and replay

Pick one moment when the game interprets an action, reports a consequence or asks the player to begin again. Write the intended experience. Observe what the player believed they were doing, what visibly changed and what meaning they took from it. Include their route and any prior knowledge. Test a changed reveal with fresh players if knowing the outcome would alter their decisions.

## Follow an action through its aftermath

A town's new evening schedule puts a repair crew and a choir in conflict over one hall. The player first examines the hall and then assigns time to each group. Later, they pass the hall on the way to the station. Write two comparable schedules, with a concrete gain and cost for each group. Give the player one chance to hear details that could change their view.

1. Ask the player what they were trying to find when they examined the hall. Before they assign time, ask what they expect each group to gain or lose. After the station scene, ask which facts or actions led to their interpretation.
2. In one version, let the game treat the inspection as a preference. In the other, base the outcome on the assignment. Keep dialogue and available actions fixed. Check when players realise their intention has been recorded. If a participant welcomes an unsettling mismatch between their intention and the verdict, ask what makes the mismatch feel earned.
3. Test the time of one piece of testimony. Play it before the decision for one group and after it for another. Ask players to name which detail they considered and when their view shifted. Good recall with the same judgment can indicate a considered choice.
4. Hold the decision and consequences fixed. Try an approval notice immediately after the assignment, then after the station scene in a fresh comparison. Ask who gained or lost and how players know. If players never saw the later consequence, trace the route and camera before interpreting what the notice did.

To check an unintended reading of the protagonist, expose one existing effect for an extra beat. For example, show that a frightened messenger takes shelter after the player shuts a gate. An improvement in factual recall supports a cue change. A player who sees the messenger reach shelter and still judges the act harshly may be responding to the action itself.

## Make a role apparent in play

- **Repeated action.** Give a partner an action the player depends on for a familiar task. Change that relationship once, such as giving the partner a shift elsewhere. Ask how the player prepares for the same task and when they notice the absence. A relationship can become legible through what both people do and how that changes.
- **Place.** Build a workshop encountered several times with the same practical use and a familiar person. Later change one feature or interrupt that use. Ask the player to guide you there and explain what they expect. Compare this with the same room seen for the first time. A title card can name a refuge. Repeated use gives players something specific to miss.
- **Commitment.** If a score rewards a choice the story asks players to question, leave room to refuse or pursue a slower alternative. Observe whether players recognise the cost and how they express their stance. For a deliberately fixed outcome, check which actions still let them express that stance and how they understand the limit.
- **Mistakes.** Let one missed input change what a character learns while the scene continues. Check whether players recognise the change and make a new plan. Replay cost and prior expectations about failure can affect whether a player accepts the event.
- **Personalised response.** Run two genuinely different input histories through the same opening and compare the game's advice or acknowledgment. Ask what players think the game inferred. When outcomes are shared across histories, the wording should reflect what the system actually knows.

## Let the player finish or begin again

Ask what the player came to achieve when they stop a timed challenge. Keep the reward they earned and offer the same route with a personal best visible. If they describe and try a different approach, look for the decision that makes mastery possible. If they say they reached their goal and leave satisfied, record completion. Ask separately whether a completion meter at the final scene changes how they interpret the ending.

For a repeating narrative, list what persists, what resets and what players can learn. Let players discover when both hall users are available, then check whether a reset lets them make a new plan using that knowledge. Compare a restart that preserves only tools with one that also preserves a clue or a useful record. Observe the new route players choose and the reason for it. New options, familiar spaces, persistent rewards and competing goals can each invite another run. Check the one your design depends on.

Keep the observed route, the player's own account, the relevant rule and the revised comparison. People can see the same facts and come to different conclusions. Identify which understanding the game needs for the next choice and where it invites interpretation.
