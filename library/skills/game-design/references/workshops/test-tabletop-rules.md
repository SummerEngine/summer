# Test tabletop rules

Build only the state that exposes the decision. Use tokens, a turn marker and a rules sheet players can execute. Ask someone to assemble the prototype and run a turn without you. Watch where they stop, who has authority to resolve the rule and what information their hands, cards or markers reveal.

## Replay a contested turn

Make a three-player market where players earn points for keeping delivery contracts. Set the closing scores before the final delivery to 7, 6 and 5. Give the trailing player a token they can spend to obstruct either other player and cost that player two points. Play the position once with the token as written, then once with a second use that earns three points by completing a final contract. Compare the two versions and try a second position where the contract is more difficult to complete.

Rotate seats and alternate the version each group plays first. Before the turn, ask each trailing player to name a move that helps their own position. Record independent options, bargaining, time to decide and who benefits from their move. Confirm that the contract can actually change an outcome. If the trailing player still mostly chooses a winner by obstructing one rival, inspect targeting power, the cost of taking the contract and when the choice is available. A negotiation game may want that leverage. Track who is positioned to profit by declining all involvement too. Waiting for others to contest the lead is a separate incentive.

Try adding one small reason to pay attention during another player's turn, such as an open offer affecting the next contract. Compare new opportunities with longer turns or a player who can settle everyone else's choices. If trade value should change as a round closes, play the same offer with five draws left and with one draw left. Record how the remaining supply changes the decision.

## Choose when chance enters

Make six draw cards. Two create a strong demand for cloth, two for tools and two for food. Give the active player three legal supply commitments. Stack the same draw order in both versions. In one version players commit before the draw. In the other they see the card first. Ask for the predicted payoff and the chosen commitment. If they always pick the same option, inspect the commitment payoffs before tuning the deck. For a game aimed at independent play by young children, quick self-resolving turns can serve the design.

For a dramatic check, compare the same roll with and without a stated threshold and stakes before the reveal. Use the same odds and consequences. Check what players anticipated and later recalled. If they understand the risk but care little about the outcome, revise what changes. Count arithmetic time as well as suspense. When comparing dice, calculate the distribution and expected value. Two dice can cluster outcomes near the centre and change the chance of a threshold result.

## Test whether players can operate the game

| Issue | Small test | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| An optional card takes longer to resolve than to choose. | Keep the trigger and intended benefit. Simplify one payment condition and give separate groups the same sample positions. | Record whether players resolve the trigger unaided. If a common position produces a different beneficiary, check balance before keeping the rewrite. |
| A stopped game is hard to resume. | Give returning players a saved position with or without a persistent marker for the next action and actor. | Compare first legal turn and time to resume. If the marker solves turn order and the plan is still lost, test a small record of the previous turn. |
| An asymmetrical power slows every turn. | Replace one repeated target reroll with a single draw from legal targets. | Compare delay and targets. Preserve the intended asymmetry and track any probability change. |
| A cooperative group shares one solution aloud. | Ask each player for a private prediction from a new position before discussing it. | Check whose reasoning transfers and whose earlier turns depended on coaching. |
| Shared fiction stalls after a technically valid response. | Run two short scenes. In the second, require each response to accept one existing detail and give the next player a concrete thing to act on. | See whether the next player can continue. Try resolving a single action versus a whole scene if authority remains unclear. |

A simulation needs a specified decision maker, scale and time horizon. Replay a known case to test the procedure, then vary a starting condition that should affect the outcome. Give the procedure to a second group unaided. Success on one case establishes what happened there. Other scenarios still need checks.

When porting to a screen, run one digital mock-up against the token version. Watch the conversation and anticipation around a reveal, who sees private and public information and how players pass responsibility. Automate one resolution at a time so a changed social effect is traceable.
