# Trace an economy through play

In a town that trades ceramics, choose a single object someone tries to buy, such as a cooking pot. Follow its value in play from the buyer's need through the shop, the kiln and the clay yard. Write down what each step uses, what it produces and what makes the step run faster or slower. A bag of clay can move from the yard into inventory. A new kiln can change how many bags become pots each day. Model those as different events so the trace shows where a delay actually enters.

## Check the first purchase and its suppliers

Start with the money and permissions a new arrival gets. Ask them to get a pot before the afternoon market opens. On the same timeline, mark when a vendor can sell one and when the arrival has enough money. The listed price can stay steady while no sale is possible. If the arrival has enough money and finds no stock, trace the clay and kiln time. If shelves stay full and the arrival cannot earn enough, trace the available shifts, wages and loans. The change depends on whether making an early connection with a patron belongs in the game.

Take the last completed sale and identify where its parts came from. Pause output from one quiet clay yard in a test copy and run existing stock down. Record who can fill the next order and how long it takes. A competing yard may cover the difference. If no one can get the needed clay, the visit count missed what the yard contributes. Test the self-sufficient maker separately from the buyer who wants a single batch. The maker may value the complete chain even when the market has alternatives.

Follow what happens when production outpaces selling. If players make pots mainly to earn practice, move the same practice credit to filling real orders for one trial. Compare unsold pots per unit of practice, completed orders and access to training for new makers. If practice becomes scarce because veterans take the orders, the surplus has been traded for a training bottleneck. In a model that returns broken items to a common material pool, players can tie up material by keeping reserves. Track what sits in stock, what is returned and what is free for new production. Try a larger pool and check whether more material reaches makers or accumulates in storage again.

## Find what an early advantage buys

In a contest between pottery yards, both players own one kiln that pays two credits each shift. A second kiln costs six. One opening leaves a player with six credits while their rival has four. The player with six can build at once, receive four credits at the end of the first shift and put the next purchase within reach sooner. The rival can buy a second kiln by the next shift if they spend nothing on orders.

Try one comparison that delays the first payout from each new kiln by one shift. The early builder now ends the first shift with two credits instead of four. If a useful order becomes available for three credits at the start of the next shift, the builder's income no longer guarantees they can claim it. Replay the same opening with exchanged starting positions. Record when each player can place the next order, the income from each kiln and whether the trailing player still has a meaningful move before the outcome settles. If the outcome is decided before the original first payout, inspect the opening contracts, stock and starting position. A short game may deliberately let one early advantage decide the rest.

## Change one market condition at a time

Two players arrive with ten credits each, and each needs one load of fuel. The first load costs three and the second costs five. Replay with the order of buyers reversed and the same budgets and needs. The extra two credits follow the second buyer in this comparison. In play, check who actually gets priority. Then check whether starting stocks make one player more likely to need a second load. The higher price can reward planning when buyers can foresee and influence their place in line.

If one port sells clay for two credits and another buys it for four, reduce travel time on one route and hold the prices steady. Count completed trips and profit in the same play window. Include carrying capacity and the opportunity cost of the trip. A price difference that supported an occasional haul may turn into the best repeatable job as the return trip gets shorter.

A paper or scripted trace shows what happens under the actions you supply. Run the smallest disputed scene with players to see whether they place those orders, pass on an upgrade or accept the risk of a long haul.
