# Action and timing

Use the creator's promise and the evidence already available. Name the skill the player is meant to show in one attempt. It could be judging momentum, anticipating a pattern, reading a cue and reacting, or sustaining a chain of accurate inputs. The same miss can matter differently under each promise. Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## Inspect the game

Reconstruct one attempt from the earliest usable cue to the outcome and the next opportunity to try. At the decisive moment, record what the player could perceive, what they tried to do, what the game registered, when the rules allowed the action, and what the feedback let the player learn. Note the state carried in from their previous decision, such as approach speed, remaining charges or recovery from a move. Locate the attempt by scene, rule or timestamp and record the relevant device, display, camera and control settings.

Compare the reported attempt with the nearest successful, teaching or lower-cost instance. A recording can show when a cue entered the frame. The player's account can establish what they noticed and meant to do. The input handler can establish the condition that accepted or rejected an input, and a replay can demonstrate one feasible sequence. An expert replay alone leaves feasibility for the intended audience open.

## Interview the creator

Continue from the creator's latest answer. Ask one question per turn and wait for their reply. Start with “What should the player be trying to do just before the miss?” when the intended response is unknown. Choose the next branch that could change the decision.

- The player chooses too late. Ask “When can they first distinguish this cue from the one that asks for a different move?”
- The player knows the response and presses it. Ask “What should happen if they press during the last moment of the previous move?”
- The player improves in practice and stalls in play. Ask “What is the first live demand that practice never asks them to meet?”
- Repeated attempts end early. Ask “What does the player have to repeat before they can test the idea from their last attempt?”
- The creator has a pitch. Ask them to act out one beat, corner or dodge at the intended pace. Use the acted attempt to identify the first unknown that needs a prototype.

## Make the decision

Pick the smallest comparison that separates the leading explanations. Change one property of the same playable moment. Compare predictions before the attempt, actual inputs and results, and what the player changes on their next attempt. A viable response and improving attempts can justify keeping a demanding window. Tune the first point where a viable, intended action breaks down. Cut a repeated segment or redundant input when it consumes attempts without exercising the decision the creator values.

Give feedback in the current turn when inspection supports a finding. Name the observation, verdict, remaining uncertainty, and the result that would change your decision. When the creator asks for an interview, follow the one-question rhythm until the evidence supports a decision or a trial would answer the question better.

## Worked diagnosis for a runner

Suppose players clear a low overhead gate reliably after two retries, then jump into it when it follows a different obstacle. The creator wants players to read obstacles while running. A correct response in the familiar sequence could come from learning when to press or from learning what the gate means. Compare first attempts on two copies of the same unfamiliar stretch, one with the original preview and one showing the gate sooner. Keep the pattern, speed, gate position and recovery rules fixed. Use fresh players for each copy. Compare unprompted actions and ask about the perceived cue after the attempt, or test prediction in a separate paused clip. More appropriate slides with the earlier cue support tuning preview distance. A clear gate still read as a jump calls for checking what the previous teaching sequence established. An intended slide that registers during the previous action's recovery calls for inspecting that state before tuning the obstacle.

If players can identify the gate and deliberately time the slide on a new sequence, keep the timing demand. If the gate reads clearly in isolation and recovery makes it impossible after one neighboring obstacle, move the gate or cut that pairing. A route designed as a memorized routine can keep a fixed sequence and use progress within that sequence as the measure. To tell recognition from memorization, put the same gate after a low step in one practice stretch and after a jump in a fresh stretch. If the player predicts the slide in both stretches but only the second rejects it, trace recovery from landing to the last safe slide input.

## Choose a worked case

- For a jump that feels wrong, an unreadable landing, or a scene whose movement should express a change in freedom, use [movement and space](references/movement-and-space.md). It also covers racing lines.
- For rhythm errors, input acceptance, access to a cue, or feedback that hides why an action failed, use [timing and input](references/timing-and-input.md).
- For practice that fails to transfer, a long retry loop, a dominant move, or a score goal that changes the reason to take risks, use [practice and retries](references/practice-and-retries.md).
