# Art direction

Start at the moment the creator cares about. A player might need to follow a child's gaze across a kitchen, enjoy the bustle of a market, or feel something move beyond the lantern. Find out what the viewer should feel, what they need to recognize in time to act, and which discovery they want to make unaided. Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## Choose a look with the creator

In an interview, ask one question per turn and use what the creator has already told you. If they ask for candidate styles or a direct critique, work from their brief and identify any assumption that affects the answer. With a named style such as “pixel art,” ask what they love about it in this particular scene. Chunky silhouettes seen from across a room and tiny changes in an actor's expression suggest different camera distances, even if both use the same pixel grid. If a reference is supplied, name a visible property and ask what about its effect the creator wants to keep. When the choice is clear, give a direction and a first study.

| What is still open? | A question that can settle it |
|---|---|
| Emotional register | “When the doors open, what do you want that first look inside to feel like?” |
| What must carry the scene | “What should I see before I move, and what would you like me to notice on the way back?” |
| Distance and medium | “Is the face a few pixels across during play, or do we stop close enough to see the eyes?” |
| Feasible technique | “Which part would you enjoy making for the twentieth room, and which part could that room reuse?” |

Build two distinct directions from their answers when they want options. Specify how to draw the same actor or object in each, how it changes when used, how the camera sees it, and one recurring cost. A choice of palette alone seldom distinguishes two production methods. Let the creator keep detail, obscurity or visual excess when it serves the moment. Give required actions a cue that survives those choices.

## Translate the direction into settings

Pick the two or three choices that actually distinguish the alternatives. Make each concrete enough for a second artist to draw the next asset.

| Visual quality | What the artist can change | What to look for in the game view |
|---|---|---|
| Shape and composition | Angular or rounded contours, relative scale, overlaps, empty space, skyline, camera height and crop | At the relevant camera distance, do the shapes overlap in a way that changes who or what the player sees? |
| Value and colour | Large light/dark groups, area held by each colour, strength of an accent, shape or label paired with a state | Does the cue survive a change of background or light? A grayscale view can expose competing values. Check the colour version with the intended audience too. |
| Surface and light | Grain size, mark direction, edge softness, highlight width, light direction, painted or moving shadows | What happens when the camera pans or a character turns? A highlight that describes a wet stone in a still can read as a moving object. |
| Motion | Poses, holds, travel between poses, loop offset, how quickly the result follows input | Watch the first use, an interruption and a repeat. Can someone tell a warning from a consequence in time? Does the motion still express the character? |
| Scale and labour | Working resolution, line weight at output, reusable tile or rig, unique poses or surfaces per object | Reduce the frame to the intended viewport. Make one ordinary asset and one awkward variant. Count the redraws, extra layers or material changes each requires. |

When several people make assets, capture the choice on a small style sheet. Include one frame at game size, a close detail showing how it was made, and a second asset that follows the same rule. Write the exception too. A painted highlight can identify polished porcelain throughout a café, while the ordinary light switch should remain visible even in shadow.

Choose a workshop by the property you need to test. Use [Playable composition](references/playable-composition.md) for the path of the eye, [Palette and state](references/palette-and-state.md) for changes in color and lighting, [Readable motion](references/readable-motion.md) for poses that communicate state or input, and [2D art production](references/2d-art-production.md) for sizing, reuse and cost.

## Critique supplied frames or footage

Name the frame, crop, camera, timestamp or asset you actually inspected. Trace the eye through the scene with visible evidence. “The rim of the empty plate is the brightest closed shape. The character's fingertip meets its edge” gives the creator something to inspect. Ask what viewers noticed when that is important. From a single frame, reserve judgments about when an event becomes visible and how reliably someone recognizes it.

Keep one successful quality. Identify a plausible second explanation for the problem. Change one property and describe how the result would distinguish them. Let the creator's actual target set the viewing conditions. Compare at play size, with the usual HUD and neighbouring scenery, under the intended camera movement. For information that matters only once, use a fresh viewer or an unfamiliar equivalent. For atmosphere, let the moment play before asking what the viewer felt. A small comparison can support the next decision even when broader audience testing is still ahead.

Return the chosen direction, a reason grounded in this game, one small study the team can afford, and what would make you revise the choice.
