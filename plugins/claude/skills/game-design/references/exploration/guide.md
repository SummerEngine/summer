# Exploration and puzzles

Use the creator's promise and the evidence already available. If called during an interview, continue from their latest answer with one relevant question and wait for their reply. If the creator wants a one-time discovery, a branching deduction, a satisfying return trip, or dread before a crossing, identify which uncertainty gives that moment its value before proposing to reduce it. Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## Inspect the game

Walk from the earliest possible encounter with the clue or obstacle to the first commitment that depends on it. For a focused review, trace the reported route and one nearby or alternate route. Record the actual version and the scene, timestamp, or rule that identifies the moment. Use what the creator supplied. A rule or a trace can establish an impossible state. A recording can establish where somebody turned or what they tried. Use an account from the player when you need to know what they intended, inferred, or felt.

Write down three sequences for the disputed moment. These can fit in a few lines.

| Sequence | Record |
|---|---|
| Reachable places | Links available in the current state, one-way transitions, return paths, and whether the player could still reach a missed clue or required object after committing. |
| Required actions | Prerequisites, which can happen in either order, and whether every solution needs the same key, clue or skill. Replay with the smallest relevant inventory and any uncommon state already supported by the game. |
| Player evidence | What can be perceived from the actual entry, view and cue settings, what it could reasonably mean at that time, what the player tried, and what the game acknowledged. Track the first point at which a plausible wrong reading could be ruled out. |

For a flooded depot, draw the route to the pump and the route back to the entrance. Then write the conditions for opening the dry exit. If the only pump handle is beyond that exit, move it or change what the door requires and recheck the route. If the player can name both the pump and the action, compare orientation, controls and the cost of return before rewriting the clue.

## Interview the creator

Choose the next branch that would change the diagnosis. Use the creator's words and the specific moment. Each row is a possible next turn.

| Latest answer or observation | Next question |
|---|---|
| “They should know to use the lens.” | “What has the player seen before touching the door that links this lens to this lock?” |
| “They say what to do, but they still get stuck.” | “What is the first command or gesture they try, and what does the game do with it?” |
| “They know which door, but take the wrong stairs on the return.” | “What could they recognize at that fork from this direction?” |
| “They get the right suspect after a few tries.” | “Which clue could they use to rule out their strongest alternative before the game gives the verdict?” |
| “It's supposed to feel like they're lost.” | “What could a player notice or choose when they decide to get their bearings again?” |
| “They just rush across instead of waiting.” | “What can a player learn from cover before deciding when to cross?” |
| “Every seed is different but runs feel the same.” | “At the first fork in two recent seeds, what would make a player choose the branch they skipped last time?” |

If the creator has an intended answer but no observations, propose the earliest plausible player reading and a competing one. Ask one question, or offer one small comparison when more design talk would leave both readings open.

## Make the decision

Choose the smallest comparison that can separate two explanations. For first discovery, use fresh players on either version or an unfamiliar equivalent with the same intended inference. For return navigation, preserve the outbound learning in both versions. For suspense and timed cues, let players make the attempt and ask about their plan immediately after it, using a timestamp if available. Cueing them to explain themselves mid-crossing changes the time and attention the encounter demands.

- **A point of no return needs timely evidence or recovery.** Check the last moment at which the player could change course. Prefer a cue there when commitment is the desired choice. Move a required fact earlier or recover it later when the story closes the route before a reasonable player can know it matters. A short, explicit time loop can make knowledge carried into a fresh attempt the intended reward. Check what a second attempt lets the player do differently.
- **Give the player a reason to move and decide what they should discover.** Start with an ordinary action that gives the player a reason to visit a place, learn a relationship, or notice a detail. Later, change what that familiar detail means. Decide whether the discovery is a goal, a possible action, a route, or a fact about the world. An immediate goal can help the player discover any of the other three. When a player requests help, start with a clue about the current obstacle and offer exact action guidance if needed. Check whether asking is possible before commitment.
- **A failed attempt can teach one thing.** Record the exact rejected action. Replay it with and without the necessary object or state. Accept equivalent actions under the same prerequisites. For an invalid idea that the scene reasonably suggests, acknowledge the target and let the reply clarify one relevant property. Keep a wrong turn if it changes the next plan and costs an amount the creator wants the player to risk.
- **A correct answer can come from guessing.** Ask for a prediction and its supporting evidence before the reveal, or give a new example that removes the remembered solution. With a generated lesson, actively look for a legal route that skips the decision it is supposed to require. An alternate solution can be the reward in an open puzzle.
- **Preserve uncertainty the player can act on.** Identify what would let a player plan, what they can afford to learn by trying, and what risk should remain unresolved. Test an earlier or more accessible cue at the decision point before specifying the entire rule. If the player already predicts the outcome and a repeat of the same relevant state behaves differently, inspect the rule and feedback before tuning suspense.

Return the observation, a competing explanation, a keep, tune, cut, add, or test-first decision, and the result that would change it. Name one thing to preserve in the comparison and what to watch for. State where the evidence ends. When the creator only asked for a design decision, give the decision and its test in the current chat.
