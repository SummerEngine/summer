# Maps and level design

A map request can mean the space the player travels through, the map they consult, or both. Start at a particular moment in play. “I want to spot my house from across the flooded town, then work out how to get there” supplies a destination, a view to protect, and a reason to leave the route uncertain. Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## Draw a first layout

When the creator asks for ideas, give them a playable proposal in the current turn. Use their setting, camera and movement. State a provisional assumption where it changes the design. If several layouts fit, offer two with different experiences. A town wrapped around a canal lets players watch their destination draw nearer from the other bank. A town built on stacked terraces can hide the destination until players rise above the roofs. Say which you would try first and what the creator gains or spends by choosing it.

- **Give the route an action.** Place the start, an immediate desire, the first decision and the destination. If the player must prepare something first, draw the order of tasks separately from the connections between places. A player can see the pier from their doorstep, reach it by two routes and still need a bell token to board. Trace a direct attempt, a detour and the trip after the world changes.
- **Choose a scale the player can feel.** Use measured movement whenever the project supplies it. Give a rough plan enough dimensions to block out one decision. For 2D, mark a standing start, run-up, reachable landing, where the camera shows it, and what a miss costs. For 3D, mark floor height, camera or eye height, and the last place the player can see or hear the branch before committing. For a world map, use travel time, travel mode, and the next chance to turn back. Identify placeholders when measurements are unavailable.
- **Spend space on a difference.** Describe what makes each route worth taking and when players can tell. A covered ramp can offer a close view of the town market. A roof crossing can offer an early view of tomorrow's destination. A detour can return to the same plaza with a shorter path home, a new piece of knowledge, or a change in the world. If the routes have the same consequence, a single well-shaped path may serve the idea.
- **Build the smallest useful blockout.** Draw connected places using an adjacency list, a labeled plan, or a short side view. Include arrows for one-way travel and conditions beside locked links. Place simple floors, walls, openings, drops and camera boundaries before spending effort on detail. Describe the route in words as well. Name one view, sound or spatial relationship that must survive the art pass.

For example, draw Post office → Bakery → either Hill steps or Quay ramp → Beacon house. The first delivery opens a footbridge home. Frame the beacon beyond the bakery on the way out and the market arch from the water on the way back. The [tide-pool mail route](references/map-cases.md#a-mail-route-around-the-tide-pool) gives the drawing, distances and both first-visit routes. The same file includes a [2D shortcut](references/map-cases.md#a-platforming-room-for-a-tired-clockmaker), a [two-floor station](references/map-cases.md#a-station-map-that-mistakes-a-crossing-for-a-connection) and an [open-order island world](references/map-cases.md#an-archipelago-that-can-be-explored-in-two-orders).

For a 2D variation, keep a sequence of inputs and draw a second surface arrangement that allows it. Compare when players see the landing and where they recover from a miss. [Design a platforming room around player actions](references/platforming-rhythm.md)

## Interview a creator

Ask one question, then follow their answer. Use a question that could change the drawing. When the creator asks for a proposal instead, draw one.

| What the creator tells you | A next question |
|---|---|
| “An island you learn by heart.” | “Which place should a returning player be able to find from a new direction?” |
| “I want the climb to feel daring.” | “At the ledge, what should make the player choose the jump over the stairs?” |
| “There are four towns.” | “Which first trip should change how the player gets between two of them later?” |
| “They should sometimes get lost in the fog.” | “When they decide to get their bearings again, what should they be able to do?” |
| “We need a map button.” | “What question will a player open it to answer at this moment?” |

## Walk an existing map

Use the available version, plan, scene, screenshot, recording or player's account. Trace the reported approach and a plausible alternate or return, starting far enough back to find the first consequential fork. At that fork record the approach direction and available movement, the cue a player could perceive, the decision they appear to make, and the cost of correcting it. Mark which details were observed, measured, reported or inferred. A top-down level plan can establish adjacency. A camera view is needed to settle whether a roof hides a landmark. A player can tell you what meaning they gave it.

Choose the first fork where two explanations make different predictions. If players turn toward the tower but take the wrong stair, moving the tower may waste the very cue that works. Test a small change to the stair entrance, with the tower and destination held fixed. If players reach the correct stair and back away, inspect how its traversal looks and behaves. Include evidence that would lead you to keep the existing design. A lost player who recognizes the canal on a second approach may be learning the place exactly as intended.

A heatmap can help locate this fork. Review individual visits alongside the trace. Pair the approach with a camera view and the player's account when the choice depends on what they saw. Record first visits and retries separately. [Test a route decision](references/test-route-decisions.md)

## Design the map players read

Pick one question and one viewing condition. A glance while moving may call for a facing marker and the next junction. A paused route-planning map may need connected floors, destinations and a legend. Preserve the distinction between **seeing a place**, **being able to reach it now**, and **knowing how to get there**. At each zoom level, retain the crossings and transitions the player needs for the decision. Try a shape, line style or direct label alongside color. Offer a concise textual route or a selectable list of exits when the spatial display is hard to use. Keep the same place and exit names in the world, map and alternative.

Test what someone can *do* with the representation. From the lower landing, ask them to find a step-free route to the platform. Watch which transition they select, whether its floor and direction are clear, and whether they can recover after facing the other way. A successful route on paper establishes connectivity. Test recognition and access in the intended viewing or listening conditions. A player can hear a destination through a wall and need a second cue to find the doorway. [Design map symbols and labels at each scale](references/map-symbols-and-scale.md) · [Test wayfinding by sight, sound and text](references/sensory-wayfinding.md)

For a design request, return a readable layout, a narrated first trip and the first cheap prototype. For critique, return the located observation, a change or keep decision, the experience to preserve, and one comparison that could change that decision.
