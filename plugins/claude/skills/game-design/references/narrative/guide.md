# Narrative and characters

Use the creator's promise and the evidence already available. For an idea, leave the creator with one playable situation and a way to try it. For an existing game, select one scene in which the player expresses a stance, acts on a relationship, or interprets what a character does. Record the scene and version, the route into it and the player's available choices. Then compare two plausible choices or visit orders. Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## Design a playable scene

1. **Start with a premise and a situation.** State what the scene explores and give the player something they can do, observe or wait for. For a conflict, give people incompatible objectives and reasons to pursue them. Give each a capability, obligation or conviction that can change the outcome. A promise between two people can supply the conflict before either needs a full biography. In a quiet scene, give the player time for a gesture that expresses the relationship. A character may let the player finish folding a long tablecloth with them even after the exit opens. Watch whether the player stays and ask what they expected would happen if they left.
2. **Decide whose story the player can shape.** Mark what the author fixes, what play can establish, and whose thoughts, events or plans the player can know. A fixed protagonist may perform an action in a characteristic way. A player-defined trait can change which action is available or how a later scene plays. Check that a common scene respects each stance the player can establish. For a limited viewpoint, find an in-world route to the information the player needs. Use a second viewpoint when seeing both sides serves the experience.
3. **Translate ambition into rules and actions.** For each person write the world state that satisfies their goal and how they can pursue it. If the role promises divided loyalties, identify the resource, deadline or commitment that makes progress toward one goal cost something for another. Give a character one action they can take while the player attends to someone else. List what the role permits, makes hard or requires for progress. Specify what players see the character actually do when they give an input. In a maintenance story, the player could choose which failing lift to visit first while the technician character carries out its familiar repair. Test whether the choice shows what each lift means to the people waiting for it.
4. **Sketch the cause and effect into the next beat.** For each action, write who can respond, what they need to know, what changes, and when the player can notice it. A stance may need a specific acknowledgment in a common later scene. A promise of lasting influence may need to change who helps, what the player can do, or which goal survives. Draft just enough of the next scene for this consequence to come up. If no response follows from a character's motives, revisit the cast, commitment or action before adding a branch.
5. **Make the first playable version around the question.** Use a situation card, one card per person's objective, action cards and a sheet for who heard what. A facilitator can apply the rules and reveal facts when the player earns them. Play from the initial situation into one response. Keep the reason to act and the cost the player weighs. If the test depends on a glance, sound, distance or timing, put that cue in a small playable scene. If a volunteer can reach a character before the player only by taking a shorter path, have two people move their pieces on a floor plan. Follow the same movement rules and see who can report the decision first.

Give the player a repair shop with materials for one job before closing. Both customers can name what they lose by waiting. Play once with a customer present for the choice and once with that customer returning after the job begins. Keep each customer's objective fixed. Write the action they can take when they learn the shop cannot serve them, such as asking for a loaner or making their own arrangements. If both jobs can finish before any commitment costs the other, tighten the capacity or deadline before adding a speech about divided loyalties.

When the role feels forced, compare the stance the game invites locally with the action it requires at the next gate. A fixed-character story can ask players how to honor a prior promise. Establish the promise and let the player see whose actions depend on it. If play has let the player withdraw that promise, carry their decision into the next scene or set up the new event that makes the character reconsider. Replay both routes and ask what players thought they had already settled.

## Trace who knows and what changes

For each consequential action, record what actually happened, who witnessed it, who received a report and when, what they believe, how they act on it, and what the player sees. Include the first chance to notice any delayed consequence. Run the same act with a witness present and absent, or the same meeting before and after the news arrives. If the response fires on both paths, check whether the character acts on a prior belief or the game is using information they never acquired.

Start with one remembered event and one response. In the repair shop, one customer sees the technician spend the last part, while the other hears only that their job is delayed. The first might ask who got the part. The second might ask if the delivery arrived. Replay after the first customer tells the second what happened. Give the second a changed response only when the report or a prior belief supports it.

Test surprise at two points. Before commitment, ask what the player thinks is at stake. Afterwards, ask what they think caused the result. A greenhouse volunteer moves a pot to a dry shelf, then later learns a caretaker took that move as a signal to water it. The retrospective connection can be the point of the scene if the response makes that reading visible. If the intent is informed responsibility for an irreversible loss, show what the shelf signals while the volunteer can still choose where to put the pot.

## Observe what the player made of it

Ask in order so the prompts leave room for the player's account.

- **Before or at commitment.** Ask what they are trying to do and what they think it could cost. For an emotional scene, observe the choice first if interrupting would change the experience.
- **After the response.** Ask what happened, how the character could know, and which moment made the response believable or doubtful. Request a specific line, action, or cue before supplying an explanation.
- **For attachment or role expression.** Ask what the character wanted, what the player wanted, and what the choice meant to them. Note whether they volunteered a relationship motive, cited a reward, or accepted a constraint they disliked. Record all cues and pressures that could explain that action.

To check how cooperation shapes a relationship, give two characters one crate they have to carry together. In one scene the companion sets it down when the player pauses. In another they continue to pull. Keep the route and rewards fixed. Ask what each character did for the other and how the interaction felt. A quick delivery can coexist with affection, irritation, or both.

If ambiguity is intentional, ask for the moment that supports each reading and the moment that makes it doubtful. Record prior knowledge, route, retries, and guide use when they could change what the player saw. A character who leaves a room without locking it could be inviting the player in or leaving in haste. Replay with both details available, such as a cleared chair by the door and an unfinished drink on the table. Test a specific clue or transition if a reading that matters to the creator has no foothold in play.

When a feature promises to recognize the player's role or intent, perform two contrary roles through the same encounter. Record controllable actions and what the game acknowledges. In a cooperative kitchen, deliver the same meal on time by checking on other stations in one run and working alone in the other. Then replay with just one assistance action changed. This can establish whether the game recognizes that act. Ask a fresh player which response made the role feel recognized and what they believed caused it.

## Check who can establish what happens at a table

Set one place and give each participant a relationship and an actionable objective. In a shared studio, one tenant wants the last drying rack cleared before pickup. The other needs their work to stay out until morning. Have each person make an offer the other can respond to, such as offering to move two trays if the other can keep one shelf. Ask what changed in the shared fiction after each offer.

Write down who declares intent, who establishes facts about the setting or another character, what rules decide the result, and who narrates it. In the studio, try resolving a proposed tray move one action at a time, then resolve an agreement in one step and let both players describe how they carry it out. Hold the initial facts and objectives fixed. If someone stays quiet, give them the first offer under the same rules. If they contribute and others build on it, test how turns are offered. If their contribution is overridden, try specifying who can establish that fact. If accepted contributions still produce no meaningful choice, inspect the available objectives or stakes.

## Worked example

Start with the premise that helping a newcomer can put a prior commitment at risk. In this proposed library story, the player's role as booking coordinator and the earlier promise to Rae are fixed. How they handle the promise is playable. Rae wants time to mount the exhibit she agreed to present. Neri needs the same final evening slot for her work to make the show. There is time for one person. Put the booking and both commitments on cards and record who hears each conversation. All routes reach the public opening the next morning, where the player's conduct can change Rae's willingness to ask for their help.

| Route | Who knows | Response the player can observe |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the promise in front of Rae and Neri | Both hear the decision. | Rae asks the player to introduce the exhibit. Neri asks for help finding another place to show her work. |
| Give the slot to Neri while Rae is away | Neri and the volunteer at the desk hear. The volunteer tells Rae before the opening. | Rae names the changed booking, asks why the player gave it away, and introduces the exhibit herself. |
| Give Neri the slot and reach Rae before the volunteer does | Rae has heard nothing about the booking. | Rae asks about her preparation window. If the player tells her and offers to help set up in the morning, Rae can ask them to come early. If the player avoids the question, Rae learns from the volunteer and introduces the exhibit herself. |

As the reviewer, replay all three rows from the normal entry point. Check that Rae uses only information she has acquired, the opening remains reachable and the response appears once at the intended moment. If saving is supported, resume just before the report and compare with uninterrupted play.

At a table, try giving each participant control of their own character's objective and reaction. Give the volunteer's player authority over when the report is delivered. Start with the same booking conflict and see whether Rae has a meaningful response in either visit order. If the volunteer can resolve the dispute alone, narrow their authority to what they witnessed and when they speak.

Use fresh players for each version of a comparison, so earlier routes do not reveal the witness or teach the consequence. Choose the change based on what players say and do.

| Observation | Change one thing | Result that changes the decision |
|---|---|---|
| Players understand the choice but cannot explain how Rae found out. | Make the volunteer's existing report visible. Keep Rae's response the same. | Keep the cue if players can name the source and anticipate the response. If they already knew the source and still find the response out of character, test Rae's reaction or establish why the promise mattered to her. |
| Players know how Rae found out but say their stance disappeared at the opening. | Replace one generic greeting with an acknowledgment specific to the choice. Keep the opening and its rewards fixed. | Keep the shared opening if players now connect the reaction to their stance. If they already made that connection and expected a concrete change in Rae's willingness to help, test one later action that depends on the relationship. |
| Almost everyone chooses Rae while saying Neri can return tomorrow. | Put Neri's existing deadline where players can see it before committing. | If players can now describe competing obligations and explain their choice, the tradeoff is visible even if most still choose Rae. If they already knew the deadline and still see no downside to either choice, change the stakes before testing more dialogue. |

For a quiet scene in the same library, start after the visitors leave. Rae is folding chairs and the player can leave, stay with her or help. Give Rae one reaction to the player staying. Ask what prompted the player to stay or leave. If players stay because they expect an unfinished task to block the exit, test a clearly open exit before rewriting Rae's reaction. Preserve the time to wait if players already understand their freedom and use it to spend time with her.

## Continue an interview

Ask one question from the creator's latest answer and wait for their reply. Start at the first gap that could change the design decision.

- For a character trait or biography, ask which action would reveal it when acting on it costs something.
- For a difficult choice, ask what the player can know before committing.
- For an apparently irrational response, ask who first saw the act and how the reacting character heard about it.
- For converging branches, ask what a returning player should be able to notice or do because of their earlier choice.
- For intended ambiguity, ask which two readings should remain possible and what moment supports each.
- For a quiet player at a table, ask what the last action they proposed changed in the shared fiction.

Use the [matching design cases](../social.md) for a genre-specific experiment. Return a verdict, the scene and route that support it, the player's account if available, the strongest competing explanation, and the smallest comparison that could reverse the verdict. Treat a trace through the game rules as evidence of possible behavior and a played route as evidence of what happened on that route. Claims about a player's feelings or reasons need their account.
