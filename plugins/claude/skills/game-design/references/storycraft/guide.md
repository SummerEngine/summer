# Story craft

Work in the creator’s medium. A short story can change what a reader understands in a single sentence. A game can let a player make the gesture that changes what a scene means. If the question concerns who witnessed a choice or how a relationship carries across scenes, also use [narrative and characters](../narrative/guide.md). Keep creator replies free of book citations and reading lists.

## When someone brings an idea

Start from the detail they care about. “My mother always left the tape running after she sang” already gives you a sound, a habit and a person. Ask one question whose answer changes what you would write. “What does the person listening do when the singing stops?” gives the creator room to imagine grief, embarrassment, boredom or delight. Wait for their answer. If they ask for a draft, write one using the details they supplied.

When someone only has a theme, offer two moments with different implications. For a story about belonging, a newcomer could correct a familiar song and hear everyone adopt the change. Or they could know all the words and choose to listen. Ask which moment feels closer, or what both miss. Build out from that answer. A scene can turn through discovery, changed attention or an accepted invitation. Use opposition when two people or commitments actually pull against each other.

## When someone brings a draft or project

Read the passage, script or sequence before asking for its premise again. In a draft, read across the scene break. A preceding line may already promise the confrontation, or the next paragraph may explain a recognition the scene has just conveyed. For a game, follow the actual entry into the named moment and its next consequence in the available project, rules or recording. Include an alternate route if it can change what a player knows. Point to the line, file, scene or timestamp. A script can establish which line can fire. Someone who encountered it can tell you what they took it to mean.

| What trouble did the creator describe? | What to inspect and try |
|---|---|
| “A lot happens but the scene goes nowhere.” | Name what someone expects on entry and the earliest act, detail or recognition that changes their next move. If three encounters do the same work, compress two and give the consequential one space. If repetition itself is the point, keep the sequence and vary what the observer notices on the third pass. |
| “The reveal falls flat.” | Read up to the sentence before it. What can a first-time reader suspect? Which question are they waiting to see answered? If the reveal is a name that nobody has reason to care about, establish a relationship. If the outcome is already clear, try letting the audience anticipate how it will come about. |
| “Everyone sounds like me.” | Give two speakers different jobs in the exchange. One wants someone to stay, another wants a clean goodbye. Write what each notices, what they avoid saying and what they do when their first approach fails. Read the exchange aloud without speaker labels. Listen for a change in tactics as well as a change in diction. |
| “The scene feels distant.” | Separate who tells it from whose attention selects the details. Try one pass from the person waiting and one from the person expected. A pause can register as the three rings before an answer or the minute spent choosing whether to pick up. Keep the position that gives the desired access and uncertainty. |
| “Players skip the line that makes the ending work.” | Trace the shortest route at the point where the line matters. Try making the necessary information part of a required action. Leave details that deepen interpretation available to discover. If the missing piece is why the player would act, rewrite the invitation and retest that route. |

For an edit, preserve the writer’s diction wherever it already does the work. Give them the specific passage, a small revision and what it buys or costs. For research-based or autobiographical material, distinguish what a witness said, what the writer remembers and what they imagine. A gap in a real account can be part of the story.

A comedy can turn on a word the writer has already used. Two guests have each said “Please” to offer the other the last dumpling. The server moves to clear the plate. Both guests grab it. Give one of them “Please” again. Does it land as a dare, a plea or a last attempt at manners? Try the same words with the other guest still holding the plate. The timing and the hands can do the work of a new punch line.

Read the workshop that fits the revision. For an opening, a promised encounter or an ending, use [Scene structure and endings](references/scene-structure-and-endings.md). For a shift in what the audience knows, use [Viewpoint and reveals](references/viewpoint-and-reveals.md). For dialogue, line timing or remembered events, use [Scene and line revision](references/scene-and-line-revision.md). For optional clues, return visits or a decision the player makes, use [Playable scene discovery](references/playable-scene-discovery.md).

## Worked revision — the space on the tape

**Before.** “Nora played the tape for her niece. Her mother had always left the last line of the song for Nora, and hearing it made her miss her mother.”

This version would work as a quick bridge into a later event. If the recognition is the event the creator wants the reader to experience, the sentence gives it away before the reader can hear the gap.

**One possible scene.**

> Her mother sang three lines. In the space for the fourth, the radiator knocked twice. Nora knew that knock. Her old room. Then her mother, close enough to brush the mike. “Any key you like, love.”
>
> Her niece reached for Stop. Nora wound the tape back four bars. This time she came in low.

The radiator lets Nora place the recording before the voice confirms who the gap was for. The two short sentences after the knock give her recognition time to land. “Any key you like” tells us how her mother invited a shy singer. Naming the feeling might suit a narrator looking back years later. Here the final change in pitch lets us hear what Nora does. Read it aloud once with “Her old room” attached to the preceding sentence. Decide whether the pause earns its place.

Try the mother’s line twice. Stress *key* if she is helping Nora find a comfortable note. Stress *you* if she is giving a child who usually follows her the lead for once. The words can stay the same. A voice actor could make either choice. On a silent page, an earlier detail about Nora singing an octave below her mother can suggest the first reading.

**One playable version.** Fix the recording and the fact that Nora is making a copy for her niece. Let the player decide what to put in the gap. On hearing the invitation they can record Nora humming, or keep the room sounds on the copy. At the end the niece puts on headphones, and the player hears the version they made. Show both actions, allow time to listen, and preserve silence as a deliberate choice. This version gives up the authored certainty that Nora sang. If singing is the ending the creator wants, fix it and let the player choose when to wind back. Then the action controls the wait.

For a captioned version, let the two radiator knocks appear in the same gap. The spools keep turning. A caption that explains why her mother paused would settle the question before the player can interpret it.

With a fresh reader, stop before Nora rewinds and ask what they think was happening in the quiet. Two supported answers can serve an intentionally ambiguous scene. With a player, ask what they expected the copy to contain, then compare it with what they heard. Someone who hears the invitation and wants to leave the gap intact has given a useful reading of the scene. Someone who waits because they never saw how to operate the recorder has identified a different revision.
