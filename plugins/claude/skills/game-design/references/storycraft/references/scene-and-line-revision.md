# Scene and line revision

## Decide what deserves a scene

Suppose two sisters are returning the keys to their late father’s flat. Edie has spent a week filling nail holes. Jo arrives on Friday afternoon to save the height marks their father scratched into the kitchen doorframe. Which part would you give a page to?

If the week’s work tells us what Edie usually does, a sentence can carry it. “All week Edie had filled the holes left by hooks, curtain rods and the little brass bell by his bed.” The last object can hurt without a flashback. Slow down where someone can still change another person’s next move. Tell the week as a pattern, then bring one encounter into focus. If a longer passage of summary still needs the texture of a particular day, give it one gesture or a line of speech. A narrator looking back may also know exactly what an event cost. Let them say it when that later knowledge matters.

**First pass.** “Edie was angry about the delay. Then she remembered how much the marks meant to both of them and decided to help.” This could bridge two scenes in Jo’s account of their lives. As the turning point in Edie’s own scene it leaves the reader to supply the event that changed her mind.

**A closer pass.**

> “He wants the keys by five.” Edie worked the lid off the paint.
>
> “Have you still got your bus ticket?” Jo held out her hand. “The back’s blank.”
>
> She put the ticket over the lowest groove and rubbed with the side of a pencil. A white seven came through the grey, with a bar across its stem. Their dad wrote all his sevens like that.
>
> The ticket slipped. Edie put her thumb on the corner.

Jo answers a deadline with a request small enough to grant. Edie can help without having to deliver a speech about their father. The sentence about his sevens **tells** the reader one necessary fact. Without it the thumb might mean no more than ordinary politeness. Try moving that sentence after the gesture. Does recognition come as Edie acts, or does a narrator explain her afterwards?

**A playable version.** Give the player Edie’s position when the ticket slips. Holding the corner steadies the rubbing long enough to read the seven. Taking the tin downstairs leaves Jo working alone. Downstairs, Edie can see graphite on Jo’s thumb when she comes out. She can only learn what came up on the ticket if Jo shows her. Both routes can reach the same bus. They differ in what the sisters did together and what Edie knows. In a linear story the writer can choose the gesture and give it the last sentence. In a game, decide whether discovering the handwriting is essential before placing it behind the choice.

## Revise the voices and the line

Give each speaker a playable verb. Edie sets a deadline. Jo recruits her. If Edie refuses, Jo might bargain for one minute, tease her about catching the early bus, or carry on rubbing with one hand. Read these versions aloud. The words change because Jo is trying something different. Eye dialect, a spelling chosen to draw attention to how someone speaks, does little to distinguish their intentions. A shared family expression can belong in both voices. Listen for who uses it to put off the decision and who uses it to close the subject. Use a gesture or a plain speech tag wherever the listener loses track of the speaker. An indirect report could let years pass in a line and leave open which sister supplied the wording.

Read “You kept my ticket” three times. Stress **you** for surprise at the keeper, **kept** for surprise at its survival, **my** for the beginning of a claim. In recorded dialogue, first play the line by itself. Listen for recurring consonants, vowels and how close the stresses fall. Then play it with the second speaker’s interruption. The emotional turn needs time to register in that mix. Try moving a short dependent clause before the last important word, then after it. Read both aloud and listen for which version gives the word room.

A poem years later might end, “I carried my father / home on a bus ticket.” The slash marks a line break. For the time it takes the eye to return to the left margin, the speaker carries a person. The little piece of paper then changes the scale of the act. Try the reverse order, “On a bus ticket / I carried my father home.” The ticket is ordinary until the second line tells us what it held. Decide which discovery the reader should make last. In a spoken version, try both a held beat and an uninterrupted sentence. A line break can hold two readings even when the speaker takes no breath there.

## When a scene comes from someone’s life

If the flat was real, ask whose account supports the detail that makes the scene work. The returned key could have a dated receipt. You might remember a mark on the frame. Your sister might remember your father writing it and place the mark on a different door. Those details carry different kinds of authority. Identify them in the draft while deciding what the audience can experience. A photograph can confirm a crossbar in the handwriting. On its own it tells us little about what your sister felt when she saw it.

In a first-person nonfiction game, making a memory playable gives a player an unusually vivid sense of having witnessed it. Give the remembering speaker language for uncertainty where the player encounters the detail. Offer the two recollections in the speakers’ own terms. If the player can try either layout, introduce them as two remembered versions of the flat. Reserve invented actions for an explicitly imagined sequence. When recording a relative, agree what part of their account they are willing to share. A writer can leave a private memory private and still write about what its absence meant.

## Try the smallest revision that changes the reading

- **The setting sounds generic.** Give the sisters the same hallway. Edie counts the holes left by coat hooks because they cost her time. Jo sees how high the last hook was because she had to stand on a chair to reach it as a child. Swap the details between speakers and listen for what no longer belongs. Repeat the test with an exact verb before adding a comparison.
- **A group exchange has one voice.** Add a caretaker at the door. Give Jo a line addressed to the caretaker that Edie will recognize as an answer to her. Ask a fresh listener who the words address and who the words are for. Use a glance or a hand on the paint lid if the second answer is lost.
- **A strong line is doing two jobs.** Read the scene once for events, once for what Jo and Edie infer about each other, and once for the timing. If the ticket scene works but the bus ticket poem explains it again, move the poem later and see whether the elapsed years give the words a new job. To use fragments, alternate the present-day handover with brief entries from a maintenance list. Let the list return only when a new entry changes how it reads.
- **The middle is packed with explanation.** Keep the sentence that establishes the deadline. Try cutting the account of the whole week down to one object and one action. Give a reader both versions and ask what they believe Edie wanted before Jo arrived. If both readings are sound, keep the pace that fits this scene. If the short version turns Edie’s choice into a surprise the creator never meant, recover one detail from the longer draft.
