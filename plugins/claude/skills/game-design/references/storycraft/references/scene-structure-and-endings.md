# Scene structure and endings

Use this workshop when the events make sense on a timeline but feel slow, abrupt or unearned in play. Draft the opening, turn and consequence as actions an audience can witness. Start with the event the creator wants someone to experience.

## Choose where play begins

Make a short chronology. Mark the first event worth doing, the first decision that needs some history, and the encounter where the consequences of that history arrive. Try starting at each mark. Playing the quarrel between two friends gives players a memory they share with them. Starting at the return of a borrowed key gives each friend room to remember the quarrel differently. A late start asks the present scene to make that past intelligible while something is happening now. Choose according to what the player should feel able to judge.

When setting up a present decision, ask who would tell its history today, what that person wants from the listener, and what the listener can now do. If all the player needs is the departure time, put it on the timetable. Give a prologue time when the occasion itself has an action or feeling worth experiencing.

## Give the player something to anticipate

Write one fact each person knows when the scene starts. Do the same for a new player and a returning player. In a bakery during a storm, a driver spreads a wet letter beside the oven. The owner arrives and asks who broke the seal. If the player saw the water wash the glue away, they can wait to see if the driver convinces her. If the player first sees the open envelope over her shoulder, the water running down the letter rack can change what they both suspect. Bring a late reveal forward in a trial draft. Keep the timing that makes the intervening actions worth hearing or doing.

Name a specific future event the player has reason to picture. Let the sound of a key turning promise a meeting and keep the visitor's response open. To see how much delay the anticipation can bear, play the scene twice. Cut one intervening exchange in the second pass. Keep the longer version if waiting makes the next line harder to say or gives the player time to act. For a replay, give the player another person to watch. Someone taking a breath to interrupt and then choosing to let a lie pass can carry a second visit.

## Give the turn an action

Check what the premise, an earlier invitation or a familiar ritual led the player to expect. If a character changes their mind, show the assurance that failed or the small piece of evidence that finally counts. An inspector arrives to sign off a bakery's stove. The apprentice names a smell from the vent and the inspector hands back the pen. Record what each person came to obtain and the first thing each does differently after that line. If information changes without changing their position or next move, the turn may belong elsewhere.

Sometimes the repercussion carries the scene. A family waiting at a table may give a predictable interview more weight than showing the interview itself. If the player has spent the last hour preparing what to say at that interview, give them a place to say it. Quiet change can also be enough. Two people clearing a table might always finish the job. Who handles the absent person's cup, and whether the other makes room on the shelf, can be enough to reward attention. Let the player linger there if that is part of the pleasure.

## Make the arrival and the close fit the choice

One chance encounter can give a comedy its starting situation. If the ending needs a succession of people to appear in just the right places, give one of them a reason to look for the others. Remove the convenient arrival from the draft. Who would go looking, who might send word, and why today? Put one of those decisions earlier. Follow the branch on which the player never sent word. Give the visitor an independent reason to appear there or let their absence have an effect.

Try closing on an immediate gesture, a later consequence or a final action the player can take. A foreman who always checked the tally can end by handing the book and the only pencil to an apprentice. A line about where to put the pencil back can give that change time to register. A later scene would earn its place if the apprentice's first use of the book is the consequence the player needs to see. Trace the shortest route and check that the ending allows enough time to perceive the last action's result.

## Revise a person, a line or a pause

- **A character feels like a device.** Give them a private reason to be in the room. If a stranger with a letter could do their entire job, combine roles or let their presence change what someone else dares to say.
- **The dialogue explains a change twice.** Write the habit the player has seen before and the first occasion on which it changes. Try the small action without the explanatory speech. Keep a line if it supplies a fact, relationship or cost the gesture alone leaves unclear.
- **The middle has become busy.** Replace one argument with ordinary work. Test whether the coming encounter stays in mind while the player pauses. If the place itself is the subject, allow a moment to hear a speaker repeat herself while the people at the back strain to hear. Try the room without the next objective on screen if the scene deserves attention of its own.

## A worked edit for a playable scene

The coastal radio station is about to air its last locally read weather report. Jo reads it. Her sister Sal phones in observations from the east pier every morning. The player works the small mixing desk between them.

**First draft.** The player tours the station's history. A visitor happens to find an old recording. It reveals that the familiar observations came from Sal. Jo explains how much the sisters' routine meant, reads the last report, and the station closes. A player who has seen the recording already has little reason to attend to the tour. The visitor does the one piece of discovering the player might enjoy. Both sisters become illustrations of something Jo says at the end.

**Rewrite.** Begin with Sal already on the line. The wall schedule gives the player one last bulletin to mix before the automatic feed takes over. In the headphones Sal says, “Can’t see the red buoy from here.” Jo checks the distance on the chart beside the desk and writes *visibility under half a mile* on her card. The player can hear what each sister contributes. While Sal checks again, Jo draws a crooked buoy beside the figure. There's a buoy just like it on yesterday's card, and another on the card underneath.

When the minute hand comes up, Sal starts her usual goodbye. Jo takes her hand off the microphone switch. “Want to say that bit yourself today?” Leave time for Sal's answer. “Happy either way. If you put me on, just the bit about the buoy.” She has agreed to a small, particular thing. The player can open the pier line for that observation and cue Jo for the rest. The listener hears wind against Sal's receiver and Jo gives her sister a clean second to finish. The player can also keep the line private and pass the report to Jo in the usual way. In that version Jo reads the familiar conversion into miles, then tells Sal on the private line that she can still see that buoy with her eyes shut. The line choice determines what the town hears and what belongs to the sisters alone. Let them both hear the result before the scene ends.

End with the player bringing down the broadcast fader. Sal used to hang up as soon as Jo began reading. Today, on either path, she waits until they have gone off air. “You coming over?” A player who knows the station will close can listen for the exact instant Sal would ordinarily have gone. The posted schedule supplies the fixed ending. The call is there because it is Sal's shift. The playable decision concerns how a shared practice will be heard for the last time.

**Test the edit.** Give one reader the ending first. Ask what they were listening for in the last minute. On the private-line path, ask another reader what only the sisters heard. If both describe the same public farewell, the mix and responses have erased the choice. If someone cannot tell who supplied the observation after missing the drawings, keep the first audible exchange intact. The drawings are a reward for attention, and the weather report must still make sense without them.

Play the shortest route and the route that stays at the desk. Check what each player could know, anticipate and influence. A returning player who already knows the outcome can still watch or listen for the instant when an old habit gives way.
