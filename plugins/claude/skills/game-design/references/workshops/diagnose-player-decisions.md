# Diagnose a player decision

In a dispatch game, the player can send a repair crew across a causeway at low tide or take a longer road. The intended decision is whether to risk missing the next crossing. Build one shift from dispatch to the result of the repair. Use a rough route map and a readable tide indicator. Finish the comparison before spending time on the title screen or drawing a second town.

## Ask for a prediction before the result

Replay the same dispatch with each route and compare the crew's arrival time and the town's subsequent power outage. Suppose the causeway and road both arrive just before the outage worsens. Their timing differs on the clock, but the player had no consequence to weigh in that instance. Put a real difference in reach before adjusting the interface.

If the consequences differ, show the existing tide and travel times beside the route choices in one version. Keep the underlying rules and starting state fixed. Watch a fresh player make the choice without coaching. Ask them to predict the outcome before dispatch, then give them a case with a different tide. Better predictions on the fresh case support a change to feedback. When everyone still chooses the causeway, move a slow maintenance tractor onto it and ask players to plan again before they drive. If they account for the changed traffic and keep the route, inspect the cost and benefit at that fork. A stable route can serve a challenge about memorizing a course.

The same comparison can surface an assumption in the model. Suppose crews assigned by neighborhood proximity always restore service faster. Keep the outage map and staffing fixed for both runs. In one, a crew has to return to its own depot for parts. In the other, crews can collect parts at any depot. Ask players which condition accounts for the different recoveries. If the results barely change, follow the rule that actually determines the outcome. Use this comparison when the player is meant to reason about causes.

## Find where an intended action breaks

A player tries to step east at the first sharp turn and the game records northeast. Capture what the player intended and which direction the game registered. Try a different rule for resolving overlapping direction inputs with the same turn, speed and animation. Fewer mismatches at that turn support a control change. When the direction matches and the avatar still catches on the corner, inspect movement and feedback.

If the player loses immediately after a new maneuver appears, put a short, repeatable opportunity to perform that maneuver just before the encounter. Use the original encounter as the comparison. Look for fewer instances of the specific error you practiced. If players perform the move and still fail at the same point, follow the next action in the sequence. A discovery puzzle can deliberately make improvement arrive in one jump.

When a mechanic works in isolation, put back the parts that may have been doing the work. For a balance challenge, place the required counterweight within reach in one version and inside the inventory in another. Keep the movement and feedback fixed. A difference in who attempts the move can expose a selection problem. If choosing among tools is part of the intended challenge, investigate the choice screen under that requirement. A paper trial can show what action people choose. Test timing and control with the intended players using the actual controls.

## Test what the activity communicates

In a neighborhood meeting scene, the player fills a support meter by assigning volunteers to seats. The brief promises persuasion, but the play rewards filling the right spaces. Keep the room, cast and decision to approve a shared garden. Try two appeals to a resident who must decide whether to let the group use her water tap. Give her a reason to care about each appeal. After play, ask which choice mattered and what the resident wanted. Explanations of her motive support the change. Talk only about the approval score directs attention back to the reward.

When the art brief calls a ceiling of overlapping shutters oppressive, give one shutter a routine that opens the balcony while blocking the stairs. See whether players start planning their crossings from what the shutters do. Ask a fresh player to point out a specific moment the room affected their behavior, before sharing the intended mood. If people see the relationship and use it but still feel at ease, decide whether the desired mood belongs in this task. Record that decision alongside the prototype. Changing the desired experience changes what the next test needs to show.
