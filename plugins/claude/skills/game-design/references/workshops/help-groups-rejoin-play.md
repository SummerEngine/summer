# Help groups rejoin play

Pick one point where people stop sharing enough information or opportunity to act together. A player may arrive late, return after an interruption, get separated in play or see other players without knowing how to join them. Start from the specific contribution you want them to be able to make.

## Restore the thread of a shared mystery

Put four clue cards about a vanished festival banner on a table. Give two people enough information to agree where the banner was last seen. Keep one question open, such as who could get it through a particular gate. Hand the returning player a fifth clue they can use to support or challenge a theory.

Give one group a short recap of settled facts, the open question and the most relevant clue. Offer the comparison group the same archive in the original order and the same new clue. Keep the other players' briefing the same. Time how long it takes a newcomer to name the open question and make a contribution the group can act on. Record what they ask the veterans for.

- If recaps speed up the first useful contribution, check who can maintain the summary when the game runs without a facilitator. Include the current objective and version of rules or clues that changed.
- If players in both groups can already state the question and get stuck on the same inference, revisit the clue or the available actions.
- If the recap reveals a discovery the newcomer wanted to make, try a short orientation with optional levels of help. Let players ask for a pointer to the relevant place before requesting an explanation. For a shared event spread over time, try a recording or digest for someone who missed a session and check what they can do on return.

## Rejoin after a route split

Draw a map for a two-person delivery. Split the players between upper and lower walkways. Give each a partial route and the same delivery deadline. Choose whether the intended play is reunion, coordination at a distance or temporary scouting. For reunion, test the same split with and without a distinctive place marked on both maps. Give the stop enough time and space for waiting.

Observe whether each person identifies the place, reaches it and resumes joint play. If both recognise it but arrive too far apart, check route length and when the clock encourages them to leave. If one person's round ends before the reunion, try a useful remaining role, such as passing along an observed landmark. Test the variant from the same saved split. Players should be able to recover from a missed meeting by reading the current state and agreeing on a next action.

## Turn contact into an invitation

When a lobby reports nearby or available players, test whether a player can tell who is asking them to play, what the activity requires and how to accept or decline. Add one explicit invitation to a prototype in a state where both players can see each other. Observe who understands it and whether accepted invitations produce a first shared action. If players understand and decline, ask what they expected the activity to demand. They may have come to watch, exchange information or finish something alone.

Repeated sessions can develop shared signals, rivalries and favours. Try the same short match with a recurring group and with reshuffled groups before attributing a change to balance. Ask what the players inferred from the current board and what they inferred from past play. An indicator that seems clear to a regular group may need a more explicit cue for newcomers.

Write down the interruption, the information or opportunity each player had, the first useful shared action and the change that made it possible. If play deliberately depends on losing contact, assess whether players still have a meaningful action while apart.
