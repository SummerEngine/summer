# Make character choices matter

In a night market, Meera repairs clocks for a living. Her cousin needs a working timer for the bread ovens by dawn. Her former teacher needs a matching part to repair the market's public clock before the morning shift. The player has one part and can complete one repair tonight. Use an encounter like this when the player can repeat the character's biography but has trouble explaining whose priorities they acted on.

## Give the role an action

Pick a moment the player already has to complete. Replace opening a storeroom with diagnosing the jammed mechanism in its time lock. Use the same facts available in the inspection tool elsewhere in the game. Let the player work out that the lock stops at the same tooth each time and free that tooth with the existing release tool. Keep the outfit and opening dialogue. Ask a new player what work they think Meera does and which event suggested it. Players who cite the inspection have inferred something from their own action. If they call it a generic lock puzzle, make the diagnostic clue or repair technique specific to the work.

Check what repeated success expresses. If every contract awards a time bonus for skipping inspection, speed defines a successful repair. Try making a working test the criterion for one contract. Ask players what they began checking before leaving the bench. For a fast arcade game, the inspection may cost more in pace than it adds to the intended role.

Goal comprehension, motive and approval are distinct observations. Let the player see an optional reaction when Meera discovers the workshop will lose its standing at the market if the public clock stays broken. Keep payment fixed. After the choice, ask the player to describe Meera's aim and recall their own reason for acting. Ask how the result feels from each point of view. A player can understand Meera's fear and still choose to help her cousin. A disagreement they can explain may be the response the scene needs.

## Trace who can know what

At a shuttered stall, a keeper recognizes that a lock has been picked. If he blames Meera, give him grounds for suspicion. On a card for each character, write what they witnessed, what they heard and what they infer. Store the incident separately from their conclusion. An apprentice who saw Meera leave with a tool can say so. The keeper might treat that as proof, while the cousin might ask what else the apprentice saw.

Start with a single contextual response at the next meeting. Repeat the same action with and without the keeper in sight. Ask the player to predict how he will greet Meera in each case and why. Then test the report itself. In one version the player can hear the apprentice tell the keeper. In another the apprentice has yet to speak with him. If players predict the right person will react and still call it arbitrary, investigate the size of the consequence.

A witness who changes position after the player commits can make even a clear risk feel arbitrary. Give the witness one stable position during that decision, with the same later consequence. Ask what caused the failure and what the player would try again. Keep moving witnesses when noticing or timing their routes is the intended activity and the player can act on what they observe.

## Give both loyalties a cost

The bread ovens support the cousin's first day running the stall. The public clock tells workers when the courtyard opens. The one part makes the commitment visible. Before the player chooses, ask what each person stands to lose. Afterward, ask why waiting until tomorrow was acceptable for one of them. If players give the same answer because one repair pays substantially more, match the pay for a comparison. Leave each relationship's cost in place.

An ally can stay loyal and still shape play. The cousin knows who keeps a spare ratchet while Meera can fit it. Give each something the other needs in one shared scene. A fixed bond can then affect what the player does during the repair even if the story never asks them to end it.
