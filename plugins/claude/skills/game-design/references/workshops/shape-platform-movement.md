# Shape platform movement

Build a plain avatar and one short route. Record controller settings, takeoff speed, hold duration, highest point, time to land, midair control and landing recovery. Keep a copy of the route for comparisons. Decide what the player should notice or attempt before giving the movement a name.

## Give one jump a character

Make a courier carry a rigid paper parcel across a printing shop. Start with a level floor and a broad landing. Try a jump with a deliberate rise, a sharp fall and one useful midair correction. Ask players to show the jump again and describe where it felt easiest to change their mind.

Use two small comparisons:

- Toggle a trailing effect that visually links the character to the floor. Preserve input, trajectory and sound. If takeoff still feels delayed, measure the time from input to motion and inspect the acceleration.
- Give separate groups the identical base jump. Offer one group a floatier alternative on a separate control. Offer the other an alternative close to the base. Match the room and exposure, and ask each group to describe the base jump freely. A shift in descriptions can reveal what the alternatives make noticeable.

Make a safe approach where a player can try a tap, a hold and a running jump. Keep the next gap identical. Check whether players alter their takeoff or hold on the first attempt. If they predict the landing and still miss, inspect input timing, runway and collision bounds.

## Turn a change of pace into a route

Draw three shop locations as the route goes from exposed to sheltered and then open again. Use ceiling height and lateral clearance to change how freely the player can move. Place a side event on a branch, then ask players where they felt free to improvise and what prompted them to leave the main path.

- For a continuous run, verify the timing without pickups. Then place the same pickups along the playable arc. Track stops, takeoff positions and landings. Players following the arc who still stop or miss may need different spacing or timing.
- For a drop, mark the last frame where steering can change the destination. Move the camera reveal earlier in one copy of the room. Ask players to indicate the landing before committing. Early accurate predictions with continued misses point to steering or reach. A deliberate plunge into uncertainty can keep its late reveal.
- Put one optional route at a fork players can read before committing. Let a player try a different stance or movement style through the same room. Check what each route costs, including missed opportunities and the reset time after failure.

## Give a creature and a secret a purpose

Sketch a shop that treats the press as worth preserving. Make six enemy behaviours across two positions. Some keep anyone away from the press. Others share it but reserve access at certain times. Give each behaviour a reason that changes the encounter. For example, a critter may retreat when approached to protect its wet print, while another moves when the player gives it a wide berth. Test whether players can vary their approach and predict the reaction. If they can predict it and still describe a timer, check whether the presentation gives any sense of what the creature cares about.

If a route designed for speed becomes a place to farm recovery items, keep the first encounter and respawns. Award the item once per visit in a test copy. Compare reversals, arrival health and what the player says they went back for. Repeated returns without a drop can mean the encounter itself is enjoyable or the route is unclear.

Imagine the press operator needs somewhere for finished prints to cool. Add a sheltered recess that rewards a pause when the main course rewards haste. Give players an observable reason to try waiting, such as a slowly clearing vent. Track who predicts the opening and who finds it by accident. Ask what the space implies and carry that implication into a second room. Keep the main route playable in both versions.

Record the route, the changed variable and the response that would lead to a different edit. Test newcomers and practiced players separately when the intended reading depends on conventions or speed.
