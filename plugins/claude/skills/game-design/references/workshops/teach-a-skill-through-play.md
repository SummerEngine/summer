# Teach a skill through play

Write what the learner should be able to do, with which information and under which conditions. Write the game's objective separately. Completing a delivery is a game objective. Choosing a viable route from a forecast and defending the choice with relevant evidence is a learning outcome.

## Turn the outcome into a paper prototype

Invite a subject specialist, a person who can represent the learners' needs and someone who can change the game rules. One person can cover several roles. Have each person propose a common error and the behaviour that would distinguish a good decision from a lucky result.

1. Choose one decision and one consequence. Sketch the learning action, the situation that makes it matter, the rules that resolve it and the controls or cues the learner needs. Write one feasible variant and leave space to revise after playing it.
2. Check prerequisites. Ask a learner to read one example forecast before the route choice. If interpreting an icon is assumed knowledge, offer a temporary reference and observe whether access to the target decision improves. Keep any reading, motor or timing requirement that belongs to the actual skill.
3. Play through once as a learner. Record what you can see, what you do, which effects you can perceive and what the designer can observe separately. Give feedback on the condition or consequence the learner should use next time.
4. Try to win without the target action. Check whether colour, card order, exhaustive guessing or another participant's help can substitute for the reasoning. One successful bypass is enough to recheck a claim that a skill is required. When the design permits useful alternate strategies, record what each strategy demonstrates.
5. Change only the suspected barrier, then play again. Record decisions the paper layout cannot show, such as whether the learner can change their plan after feedback. Check feasible session length, inputs and any account or setup requirements before implementation.

## Try a forecast and a fresh case

Draw two routes for a 3 m tall ferry that must arrive by noon. The bridge route takes 30 minutes. The detour takes 50 minutes. One forecast gives 3.5 m of clearance and another gives 2.5 m. At an 11:20 departure, the first forecast makes the bridge the only viable route. At an 11:00 departure, the second makes the detour viable. Include a case where both routes work. Give the learner a choice before showing whether the ferry clears the bridge and arrives on time.

Check for a colour shortcut by swapping only the route colours and asking for the choice and reason before showing the outcome. If choices follow the colour, remove that cue from both versions of the next test. Compare a practice round that asks learners to select relevant evidence before committing with one where they can decide directly. Hold the forecast, timing and feedback fixed. Compare learners with similar starting skill and give them equal practice time. Set the scoring rule before play. Credit the decision and the reason separately. Accept either route when both meet the constraints and the learner can explain the choice.

Give everyone a new case without the practice prompt. This case might require using a clearance table at a different crossing. If scores improve only on the practiced case, vary surface details and check whether the first feedback explained how the rule worked. If learners give the right answer aloud but act too late, inspect whether they saw the change before committing. When the learning claim concerns a later or real task, plan a separate comparison there.

## Swap the practice activity to fit the skill

| Target skill | Practice comparison | New case |
|---|---|---|
| Intercept a telegraphed move. | Use an opponent that actually attacks and shows a successful intercept. Match time with a group rehearsing controls on a stationary target. | Use a different telegraph with the same defensive rule. Check detection, timing and execution separately. |
| Recognise a unit's counter in a tactics game. | Offer either a short exercise with the counter or a neutral exercise of the same length. Use different terrain in both. | Keep both groups' starting forces and map identical. Look for independent selection and use of the counter. |
| Move the hand to a new group of controls in a rhythm game. | Compare a sparse phrase with a hand shift and a phrase of equal length at the original position. | Match speed and use an unfamiliar phrase. Check whether missed notes cluster near the shift. |
| Distinguish a weak joint from a part buckling under load. | Toggle a connection preview on the practice structure. | Give everyone the same materials and feedback on a new span. Ask for a prediction and one repair. |
| Apply a rule during group play. | Ask players to record their own prediction before group discussion for one round. | Give each player an unseen position. Check individual choices and reasons even if the group played fluently. |

Hand off the learned action, the original and changed practice conditions, the reasons learners gave and the fresh-case results. Keep evidence about enjoyment, independent performance and use in other settings distinct.
