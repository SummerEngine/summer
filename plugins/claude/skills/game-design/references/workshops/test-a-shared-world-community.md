# Test a shared-world community

At a volunteer radio station, three players can cover a weather shift together. They can also run the drills alone. A successful shift followed by no solo replay leaves several explanations open. At the next visit, offer the same short drill alone or with a familiar partner. Ask what drew each player to their choice. A return with a partner and an account of something they did together support the social explanation. Declining both options can reflect fatigue, the time commitment or being satisfied with one visit.

## Watch a group form and reform

Give two comparable groups the same short field trip. Offer one a private channel that closes when they return. Record whether an invitation becomes a departure and whether anyone feels pressured to join an existing crew. Ask whether they came to recruit regulars or go out with tonight's company. If they already have a channel and still hesitate, watch how they agree to start.

For a member returning after an absence, offer the option to rejoin at the group's current lookout. Keep credit and reward fixed. Measure time to their first shared task and later sessions with the same companions. Include absent and nonreturning members in the record. If groups reunite faster but their members still drift away, investigate what the group can do after everyone arrives. A fresh supply of newcomers can make the station look busy while established groups disappear.

During a shift, write down a term the regulars use naturally. When a regular says “take second,” ask a newcomer to show what they would do next. Give another newcomer an explanation at the point where the phrase is used. If they can explain the phrase but still cannot tell whose turn to transmit it is, try a visible cue for turn ownership. Let the crew establish which terms are worth explaining before building out a glossary.

## Give contributions and roles a purpose

Players can build and deliver a weather packet but seldom make a second one. Keep the construction and delivery steps. Give one receiver an actual departure choice that depends on the wind reading in the packet. After the first required delivery, watch for voluntary ones and ask the sender what they thought their delivery changed. If the consequence is understood and deliveries still take too much time, look at travel or whether a receiver is usually around. Preserve the recipient's reason to read the packet when simplifying the prototype.

Check whether a role can complete its own basic activity. A player who chose to maintain the station has to cross an exposed field to deliver replacement batteries. Give them safe passage on the first trip while holding the repair cost and reward steady. Watch for first deliveries and voluntary second orders. When players complete the trips but stop at the bench, examine the work the bench offers. For a crew whose goal is hazardous escort, the field may be the reason to make the trip.

Gifts from a veteran can also change the work a newcomer sees. List the choices an early drill is meant to teach. Compare newcomers with standard and donated receivers, then give both groups standard equipment for an unfamiliar assignment. Better first-shift results followed by weaker independent performance can expose a skipped decision. If both groups can diagnose the next fault, the gift made entry easier without that loss.

## Ask who the activity serves

A veteran spends their shift helping newcomers and uses their next evening to chart remote repeaters. Ask about both evenings and what they would do with another free one. The help log alone can make two people with different interests look alike.

For a player-built listening room, ask the maker who should visit and what they hope will happen. If it is a gift for their old crew, watch whether invited friends can find it and recognize the recordings meant for them. If it is a public listening puzzle, see whether an unbriefed visitor can find the room and work out how to use it. A handful of visitors could satisfy the first goal. The second requires checking where strangers stop.
