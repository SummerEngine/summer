# Test access through play

Choose something the player wants to do, such as navigating a course with a friend or directing a crew during one encounter. Ask which inputs, outputs and settings they normally use. Agree on what a satisfying contribution looks like to them. Start with their setup and let them set the pace and decide when to stop.

## Follow one complete trip

1. Begin at launch. Ask the player to find and set their options, reach the intended activity, take a turn, recover from a mistake, leave and resume. For a social game, include accepting an invitation, signaling readiness and communicating during play.
2. Record the first blocked step. Note the action, available controls, focus or pointer position, the cue the player could perceive and the time available. A reachable button can still be useless if focus never reaches it. A cue can be readable after the decision window closes.
3. Change one requirement and repeat the journey with an equivalent new encounter. Count independent actions and unintended activations. Ask about effort after several repetitions. A player can hit every target and still find the session exhausting.
4. Repeat with saved settings after a restart. Inspect settings conflicts, reassignment of critical controls, recovery from a lost connection and whether an assist works in menus, play and the pause screen.

A developer can find forced inputs with a keyboard-only, pointer-only, sound-off or flat-device pass. Use these passes to find barriers to investigate with players in their usual setups. For a physical card game, check how the player identifies private cards independently as well as public state.

## Make one crew role playable

On a floating greenhouse, one player moves a lift and a partner controls shutters. The partner watches the load sway and picks the moment to release it. Draw a three-stop lift route and prototype one trip. Use the same three stops for each version.

- **Entry.** Give both roles the same reachable actions for Join, Ready and Back. Test that a switch user can enter from launch, play the role and leave with the same setup. If Join works and Ready stays unreachable, trace focus before adding another binding.
- **Control.** Try a single activation that commits a release. Keep the timing choice and show the partner what their action changed. Ask which release they chose and whether they want another turn. If the trip succeeds but the player feels incidental, test the role's contribution separately from its input count.
- **Cues.** If colour signals sway, compare a version with a distinct shape at the load. If a bell signals that the shutter is ready, test a timed visual alternative in a separate pass. Test with unfamiliar loads and sound off. Ask what changed while the player can still respond. If symbols read in isolation but overlap in play, fix their placement.
- **Timing.** Try a slower private round with the same action order and consequences. See whether the player detects the cue, chooses a response and executes it. For shared play on one clock, try earlier cues, queued input or a private practice round. Keep the shared outcome intelligible to both roles.
- **Recovery.** Test a missed release and return from a pause. Offer a nearby retry or a practice trip if the player wants to try again. Ask what would let them keep playing with their partner.

## Select the smallest useful change

| What happens | Compare | What to observe |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory selections are accurate but repeated use tires the player. | Move the frequent action beside its item. Keep sensitivity, target sizes and task fixed. | Compare travel distance, errors and effort. If effort stays high, inspect hold length or the number of confirmations. |
| A map reader knows the rules but confuses two markers. | Add distinct silhouettes to the same positions and scale. | Ask them to identify unfamiliar examples under their usual viewing conditions. Check crowding separately. |
| Dialogue is readable but an event outside the view goes unnoticed. | Add a timed caption or cue with the event and any useful direction. Include speaker identity and tone where dialogue needs them. | Check whether the player has enough time to act while attending to the encounter. |
| A phone player misses small controls or activates one twice. | Test larger hit areas and tap handling in separate passes. Try an alternative to a required tilt or simultaneous gesture. | Track misses and extra activations independently. Confirm the game still receives deliberate repeat taps. |
| A reader can follow the menu but loses the objective during play. | Put a short readable objective where the player checks status. Try scaling or spoken presentation if the player needs it. | Ask them to identify the next useful action unaided in a new situation. |

Report the journey, setup, first barrier, changed requirement and observed contribution. Confirm separately which combinations work, for example a remap with the slower practice clock and a larger interface. Ask the player whether the result serves their reason for joining.
